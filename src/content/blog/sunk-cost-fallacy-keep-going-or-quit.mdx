You have paid for the course, and you hate it. You have spent $2,100 on repairs for a car that just broke again. You have been in a project, a degree, a job or a relationship for years, and some quiet part of you suspects it is not going anywhere. Then comes the thought that keeps you stuck: *but I've already put so much into this.*

That thought has a name, the sunk cost fallacy, and most articles about it stop at the definition and a few examples. That leaves you with a problem. "Ignore what you've already spent" is easy to say and hard to apply, because real situations are messier than the textbook version. Some of what feels like a sunk cost is not sunk at all. Some persistence is rational. And sometimes the person shouting "sunk cost fallacy" is the one making the mistake.

This guide gives you a practical way to sort it out. You will learn a one-question test for what is truly sunk, a "forward ledger" you can fill in with real numbers, kill criteria you set *before* you are emotionally attached, and what the research does and does not show. It is written for everyday decisions about money, time, work and relationships.

## The Short Answer

- **The rule in one line:** a cost that you cannot get back, whichever way you choose, should not decide what you do next. Only what happens *from here on* should.
- **Use the quit-today test.** For any number in your head, ask: *would this change if I quit today?* If yes, it is a real future cost or benefit, so count it. If no, it is sunk, so leave it out.
- **Many things that feel sunk are not.** Cancellation fees, commitments to other people, and what you would still need to spend to finish are all future, and they belong in the decision.
- **Ask the fresh-start question.** If you had none of the history and were offered this exact situation today, would you choose it?
- **Quitting can be the mistake too.** The middle of anything worthwhile often feels like failure. Decide your exit conditions in advance, so a bad week does not make the call.

## What the Sunk Cost Fallacy Actually Is

A **sunk cost** is money, time or effort that you have already spent and cannot recover. The **sunk cost fallacy**, also called the sunk cost effect, is letting that past spending steer a decision about the future. Its sister concept, **escalation of commitment**, is what happens when people keep adding resources to a failing course of action. The Concorde supersonic jet is the classic example: the British and French governments kept investing in it despite expectations that it would not pay off commercially.

The logic for ignoring sunk costs is simple. If you cannot get the money back whether you stay or leave, then it is the same in both branches of the decision, so it cannot tell you which branch is better. What differs between the branches is the future: what each path will cost you from now on, and what each will give you.

Here is why it is still hard in practice. Real decisions do not arrive labelled. The next section is the missing step.

## Step One: Is It Really Sunk? Run the Quit-Today Test

Take each thing you are worried about wasting and ask one question: **if I quit today, would this number change?**

| What you are thinking about | Would it change if you quit today? | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I paid $600 for this course, non-refundable." | No. It is gone either way. | **Sunk. Ignore it.** |
| "I've spent three years on this degree." | No. The years are done either way. | **Sunk. Ignore the years.** |
| "I'd lose my $400 deposit if I back out." | Yes. Quitting would trigger the loss. | **Not sunk. It is a future cost of quitting. Count it.** |
| "I'm two modules from finishing." | Yes. What you still need to spend to finish and what finishing gives you are both ahead of you. | **Not sunk. Count the remaining cost and the remaining benefit.** |
| "My co-founder and two employees are counting on this." | Yes. Quitting changes what happens to them. | **Not sunk. A real future consequence.** |
| "Everything I learned doing this." | No. You keep it either way. | **Sunk, but also an asset. It helps you whatever you pick.** |
| "I'd have to admit to everyone I was wrong." | Yes, partly. There may be a real reputational cost, and there is also a feeling that fades. | **Split it. Count the real cost, discount the discomfort.** |

Two things stand out when you run this honestly.

**First, the test rescues you from the opposite mistake.** If you only ever chant "ignore sunk costs," you will wrongly dismiss deposits, penalties, promises to other people, and the value of being almost finished. All of those are future-facing and legitimate.

**Second, the "I'm almost done" argument is the sneakiest.** Being 90% finished is a reason to continue only if the *remaining* cost is worth the *remaining* payoff. Finishing something not worth having is just a more expensive way to finish it.

## Step Two: The Fresh-Start Test

Imagine you have none of the history. You did not spend the money, the years or the effort. Someone offers you this exact situation as it stands today, with its current prospects and its remaining costs.

- **Would you choose it?** If a flat "yes," you probably have a real reason to stay, so name it.
- **Would you pay the same price to enter today?** If you would not buy the course, take the job or start the project knowing what you now know, you are staying for the past, not the future.
- **What would you tell a friend?** Research on [self-distancing and wise reasoning](https://uwaterloo.ca/wisdom-and-culture-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/grossmann_kross_-_exploring_solomons_paradox_-_article_proof.pdf) by Grossmann and Kross found that people reason more wisely about other people's problems than their own. Describe your situation as if it were someone else's, in the third person, and see what verdict you give.

A useful trick is to rewrite your reason for staying and see if it still stands. "I should stay because I've already spent two years" becomes "I should stay because the next two years will be better than my alternatives." If the second sentence feels false, you have your answer.

## Step Three: The Forward Ledger

Feelings argue louder than spreadsheets, so write it down. Use only three columns, and only future numbers.

| | Keep going | Stop and do the best alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cost from today** (money, time, effort, including penalties for quitting) | | |
| **What I realistically get** (and when) | | |
| **How confident am I?** (low, medium, high) | | |

The "best alternative" matters. Continuing is never compared with nothing. It is compared with whatever else you could do with the same money, hours and energy. Economists call that **opportunity cost**, and it is the cost most people leave out.

### A worked example: the car that keeps breaking

These numbers are hypothetical but typical. Your car needs an $1,800 repair. This year you have already spent $2,100 on fixing it. A mechanic says you should expect around $900 a year in further repairs, and the car would probably last two more years. A comparable replacement costs about $6,500, and you could sell the current car for $1,200.

- **The $2,100 you already spent:** sunk. It appears nowhere in the ledger.
- **Keep:** $1,800 repair plus about $1,800 in repairs over two years, which comes to roughly **$1,800 a year**.
- **Replace:** $6,500 minus $1,200 is $5,300 net. Spread across a five-year life, that is about $1,060 a year, plus perhaps $300 a year in repairs, so roughly **$1,360 a year**.

On these assumptions, replacing is about 25% cheaper per year. The decision turns on the future figures. And notice the honesty built in: you must estimate repair costs and lifespans, and those are uncertain. If the two options come out within about 20% of each other, treat the numbers as a tie and let other factors decide, such as hassle, safety, or how much cash you have right now.

## How It Plays Out in Real Life

The table below sorts common situations by what is sunk, what to count instead, and the trap that catches people.

| Situation | Sunk (ignore) | Count instead | The common trap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Old car, big repair** | Past repairs and what you paid for the car | Future repair odds, annual cost vs replacement, safety | "I just fixed the transmission last year." |
| **Degree or training you no longer want** | Tuition already paid and semesters done | Remaining cost, what the credential unlocks, other routes to your goal | "I can't waste four years." |
| **Side project or startup** | Hours, savings already invested | Evidence of traction, cash runway, the best other use of your time | Treating effort as proof of promise |
| **Subscription, gym or course you don't use** | What you've already paid | Whether you will use it from now on, and cancellation terms | "I'll get my money's worth eventually." |
| **Mobile or gacha game, ongoing spending** | Everything already spent | Whether the next hours and dollars are enjoyable on their own | "I'm so close to the rare item I paid for." |
| **A bad film, book or meal** | The ticket, the first 100 pages, the entrée price | How you want to spend the next hour | "I've already paid for it." |
| **Home renovation that ballooned** | Money already in the walls | The cost to finish vs the cost to sell as it stands, and what you will actually live with | Spending more to "justify" the earlier spend |
| **A stock or investment** | Your purchase price | Whether you would buy it today at today's price | Waiting to "get back to even" |

The hardest case, relationships, deserves its own section.

## Relationships: Years Together Are Not a Reason Either Way

"Sunk cost fallacy" gets used loudly in conversations about relationships and careers. Handle it with care, because here the framing can mislead in both directions.

**What is sunk:** the years, the shared history, the energy spent. Those are real, and they are also done. They do not tell you whether the next five years will be good.

**What is not sunk, and deserves weight:**

- Whether the problems are **changing**. A rough patch with effort and progress differs from the same fight repeated for years.
- **Other people**, such as children, who will be affected by what you decide.
- **Practical costs of leaving** and the real support you have.
- **Genuine value** you still get: warmth, trust, shared goals.

A better forward question than "have I invested too much to leave?" is: *"If I imagine the next five years, is a future with change likely to be better than a future without it, and what has actually shifted in the past year to make me think so?"*

You can use the fresh-start test here too, but gently. "Would I choose this relationship today?" is a heavy question, and one hard week does not answer it. If the question keeps returning over months, that deserves a real conversation with the other person or a counselor, not a ten-minute worksheet.

**If you are afraid of a partner, or are being controlled or harmed, this is not a cost-benefit exercise.** Please contact a local support service or a trusted person. This article is general information, not relationship or mental health advice.

## The Opposite Mistake: Quitting Too Soon

Over-correcting is a real risk. Labeling every hard stretch a "sunk cost trap" makes you someone who abandons things at the first dip. Many worthwhile efforts, such as a language, a business, a craft or a fitness habit, feel worst somewhere in the middle, when the novelty is gone and the results have not arrived.

So when is a rough patch a signal to leave, and when is it just the middle? Ask:

- **Is the difficulty information or just discomfort?** "Customers don't want this" is information. "I'm tired and it's slow" is the middle.
- **Is the trend improving, even slowly?** Flat for a long period with no change in approach is different from a slow climb.
- **Would my future self, who knows how this turned out, call it patience or stubbornness?** You cannot know, which is why the next section matters.
- **Am I being honest about the evidence?** Persistence can be rational when continuing generates new information or when stopping would be very costly. Economists McAfee, Mialon and Mialon argued in [a 2010 paper](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/ecinqu/v48y2010i2p323-336.html) that taking sunk costs into account can sometimes make sense, for example when a failure tells you something about what is still needed, when budgets are limited, or when reputation is at stake. Treat that as a reason for care, not a free pass: in most personal decisions, "I've already spent so much" is not one of those reasons.

## Set Kill Criteria Before You're Attached

You cannot judge your own project fairly once you are emotionally invested. So make the decision to quit, or to continue, **in advance**, while you are still calm.

Decision researcher Annie Duke makes this a central idea of her book *Quit*, where she calls such conditions "kill criteria": pre-committed signals that tell you to stop. The form is simple. Write them down at the start:

> **Project:** Launch a paid newsletter.
> **I will stop if:** After 6 months I have fewer than 100 paying readers, **or** I have spent more than $1,500, **or** I dread working on it for three weeks in a row.
> **I will review on:** 15 March, with one person I trust.
> **What I'll do instead if I stop:** Move the time to the consulting work that is already paying.

Why this works:

- **It splits the decision from the mood.** The "you" who wrote the criteria was thinking clearly. The "you" six months later, in the middle of it, gets a signal rather than a debate.
- **It limits the downside.** A spending cap and a deadline mean you cannot sink in endlessly.
- **It protects persistence too.** If the criteria are not met, you have written permission to carry on without agonizing, which is the cure for the quitting-too-soon problem above.
- **A trusted second person helps.** Someone with no history in the decision can see the fresh-start question more easily than you can.

For money, the equivalent is a **stop-loss**: "If this goes below X, I sell," decided before you buy. For a purchase, it is a **return window**: set a date to decide, then honor it.

## What Research Says (and Where It's Murky)

The sunk cost effect is well known, but the evidence is more mixed than the popular version. Here is a fair summary.

**The classic evidence.** In a 1985 paper, Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer ran a field study with a university theater. Season-ticket buyers were randomly assigned to pay full price or one of two discounts. According to [a summary on Gwern's research notes](https://gwern.net/sunk-cost), full-price buyers attended more plays in the first half of the season, and the difference was not significant in the second half. A later review by Friedman and colleagues noted the result was weaker than it looks. The study is famous partly because the story is so clean, not because it is airtight.

**Responsibility makes it worse.** Barry Staw's 1976 study, "Knee-deep in the big muddy," found that people committed more resources to a failing course of action when they had been personally responsible for the original decision. The takeaway for you: the more a decision feels like *yours*, the harder it is to walk away from.

**Age may matter.** A 2008 study by JoNell Strough and colleagues in *Psychological Science*, [Are older adults less subject to the sunk-cost fallacy than younger adults?](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2008.02138.x/), found that older adults showed less sunk-cost bias. The authors suggested one reason may be that older adults focus less exclusively on losses.

**Animals and children.** Arkes and Ayton argued in 1999 that clear-cut examples of the effect in non-human animals are lacking, and that young children appear less susceptible than older ones. They suggested adults may be influenced by a norm such as "don't waste." That is one summary of [the argument](https://conversableeconomist.com/2014/12/29/sunk-cost-fallacy-do-children-and-animals-avoid-it/), and it is not settled. A 2018 *Science* paper by Sweis and colleagues reported sunk-cost-like behavior in mice, rats and humans in a waiting task, but [later work has debated](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.10.07.462802.full.pdf) whether that behavior reflects true sunk-cost sensitivity.

**What to take from all of it:**

- The pull of past investment is real and common, and a clean theoretical rule exists for it.
- Effect sizes in careful studies are smaller and more context-dependent than internet summaries suggest.
- Some "sunk cost" behavior has rational parts, such as information, reputation and costs of stopping, which is exactly why you split the real from the sunk with the quit-today test rather than banning all consideration of the past.

## Why It's So Hard to Let Go (and a Counter for Each)

| What your mind says | What's going on | Counter-move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Quitting means it was all wasted." | An aversion to waste. Arkes and Blumer proposed this norm in 1985. | Ask: is continuing the *less* wasteful path from here? Spending more adds to the waste. |
| "I chose this, so it must be good." | Responsibility effect, as in Staw's study. | Have a neutral person assess the next step. |
| "I'd have to admit I was wrong." | Wanting to look consistent. | Redefine it: "I decided with what I knew. Now I know more." |
| "It's so close to working." | Optimism about the remaining distance. | Estimate the remaining cost **and** the payoff in writing. |
| "I can't stand being the one who quit." | Identity and story. | Write the story as a *redirect*: what you learned, where the time goes now. |
| "Maybe if I wait a bit longer." | An open door that never closes. | Set a decision date and a kill criterion. |

## A Ten-Minute Keep-or-Quit Routine

1. **Write the decision in one sentence.** ("Keep funding the side project, or stop.")
2. **List what you're afraid of wasting.** Money, years, effort, pride.
3. **Run the quit-today test** on each item. Cross out what is truly sunk.
4. **Fill in the forward ledger,** including the best alternative use of your time and money.
5. **Run the fresh-start test.** Would you choose this today? Would you recommend it to a friend?
6. **Decide, or set kill criteria and a review date.** If you stay, write what would make you leave. If you leave, write what you will do with the freed-up time.

## When the Numbers Tie

Sometimes the forward ledger really is close. If your best honest estimates differ by less than about 20%, you are in tie territory, and agonizing further rarely adds information. Here a simple device can help: flip a coin, or press a yes/no button, and pay attention to your reaction. Relief or disappointment can show you what you wanted. You do not have to obey the result, and you should not use it for decisions with serious safety, health or legal consequences. For those, take the time and get proper advice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the sunk cost fallacy in simple terms?

It is letting money, time or effort you have already spent, and cannot get back, push you to keep going with something that is no longer worth it. The better question is what you will pay and gain from today onward.

### What is an example of the sunk cost fallacy?

You keep repairing an old car because you already spent $2,100 on it, even though replacing it would cost less per year going forward. Other common examples are finishing a bad meal you paid for, staying in a degree you no longer want, or putting more money into a failing project to "justify" what you have already put in.

### Is the sunk cost fallacy always a fallacy?

No. Some things that feel like sunk costs are really future costs or benefits, such as cancellation penalties, commitments to other people, or what you still need to spend to finish. Economists have also argued that taking past investment into account can be rational in specific cases, for example when a failure reveals information or reputation is at stake. The skill is separating what is truly sunk from what is not.

### How do I stop falling for the sunk cost fallacy?

Use the quit-today test: if a number would not change when you quit, ignore it. Then compare the forward costs and benefits of continuing against your best alternative, and ask whether you would choose this if you were starting fresh. Setting kill criteria before you begin makes it easier still.

### Is sunk cost the same as loss aversion?

They are related but not the same. Loss aversion is the general tendency to feel losses more strongly than equal gains. The sunk cost effect is a specific case, where past spending influences future decisions. Researchers have suggested that loss-focused thinking can contribute to the sunk cost effect, but they are distinct ideas.

### How does the sunk cost fallacy apply to relationships?

The time you have already spent together is sunk. It is not a reason to stay or to leave. Focus instead on what is not sunk: whether problems are changing, who else is affected, what leaving would realistically cost, and what good remains. If you are unsafe or being harmed, this framework is not the right tool, and you should reach out to a support service or a trusted person.

### Can I quit too soon because I'm worried about sunk costs?

Yes. Treating every dip as a sunk cost trap can make you abandon things that would have paid off. Distinguish information from discomfort, check whether things are improving, and set your quit conditions in advance rather than at your lowest moment.

### What is the difference between sunk cost and opportunity cost?

A sunk cost is already spent and cannot be recovered. An opportunity cost is what you give up in the future by choosing one path over another. Sound decisions ignore the first and always include the second.
