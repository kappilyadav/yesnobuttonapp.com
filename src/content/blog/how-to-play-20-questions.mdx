Twenty Questions is one of the few games that needs no board, no cards, and no setup beyond a bit of imagination. One person thinks of something, everyone else asks yes or no questions, and the guessers get twenty tries to work out what it is. It is a staple of road trips, classrooms, and awkward first dinners, and it is also a surprisingly good way to learn how to ask useful questions.

This guide covers the exact rules, the math behind why twenty questions is enough to find almost anything, a step-by-step strategy with a worked example, the best opening questions, ideas for what to guess, and variations for different ages and settings.

## The Rules in One Minute

1. **Pick an answerer.** One player secretly chooses a subject. Agree beforehand on the category: a person, a place, or a thing is the classic starting point.
2. **Everyone else asks yes or no questions.** Players take turns. The answerer may only reply "yes" or "no".
3. **The answerer must tell the truth.** Lying is not allowed. If a question cannot be answered honestly with a yes or no, the answerer should say so, or the group can agree to allow "sometimes" or "maybe".
4. **Count the questions.** The guessers have twenty. A correct guess ends the round, and the person who guessed it becomes the next answerer.
5. **If twenty pass with no correct guess, the answerer wins.** They have stumped the group and usually stay on as the answerer for another round.

That is the whole game. Most disputes come from two small details, so decide them before you start: whether a wrong final guess uses up a question (it normally does), and whether "maybe" is a legal answer.

## Why Twenty Questions Is Enough: The Math

Each yes or no question can, at best, cut the remaining possibilities in half. Ask a question that splits the options perfectly and you go from 1,000,000 candidates to 500,000, then 250,000, and so on. After twenty perfect questions you can tell apart 2 to the power of 20, which is 1,048,576 different things.

Computer scientists call this a binary search, and information theorists describe it as roughly twenty "bits" of information. If you want the formal background, see Wikipedia's explanations of the [binary search algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_search_algorithm) and [information entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_(information_theory)). The practical lesson is simple: **a good question is one where you genuinely do not know whether the answer will be yes or no.** A question whose answer is almost certainly "no" teaches you very little, even when you luckily get the answer you wanted.

Real games never reach perfect halves, because the world does not divide neatly. But aiming for a rough 50/50 split is the single best habit you can build.

## A Worked Example

Suppose the answerer is thinking of **a toaster** and you have no idea. Here is how a strong questioner might proceed, with the reasoning in brackets:

1. *Is it a living thing?* **No.** (Splits animals and plants from everything else.)
2. *Is it something people made?* **Yes.** (Rules out rocks, mountains, weather.)
3. *Is it usually found inside a home?* **Yes.** (Rules out vehicles, buildings, tools used outdoors.)
4. *Does it need electricity or batteries?* **Yes.** (Rules out furniture, books, clothes.)
5. *Is it used in the kitchen?* **Yes.** (Rules out TVs, chargers, lamps.)
6. *Is it bigger than a loaf of bread?* **No.** (Now we are down to small appliances and gadgets.)
7. *Does it heat food?* **Yes.**
8. *Is it used for breakfast food in particular?* **Yes.**
9. *Is it a toaster?* **Yes.** Solved in nine questions, with eleven to spare.

Notice what the questioner did not do. They never asked "Is it a stapler?" on question one. Early on, every question was broad enough that either answer would eliminate a large share of the possibilities.

## The Strategy Step by Step

**1. Start with the biggest splits.** Good openers are the questions that divide the whole universe of possibilities roughly in half:

- Is it alive, or was it ever alive?
- Is it a person, a place, or a thing? (Ask these as separate yes or no questions.)
- Is it real, or is it fictional?
- Is it man-made?
- Could you hold it in one hand?
- Would most people recognize it?

**2. Narrow by category, then by property.** Once you know the broad kind of thing, move to properties: size, color, location, purpose, material, and who uses it.

**3. Keep a mental list of what you have ruled out.** Many players forget their own earlier answers and waste questions re-checking something already settled. Write the answers down if the group allows it.

**4. Save specific guesses for the end.** A specific guess is only worth a question when you have narrowed the field to a handful of candidates. Guessing early almost always wastes a turn.

**5. Use "or" questions carefully.** "Is it a cat or a dog?" is fine only if the answerer is allowed to answer "yes" to either. Most groups treat questions with "or" as two questions, so check the house rule first.

**6. Switch to guessing around question fifteen.** If you are still unsure at that point, start naming your best candidates. Four or five questions is enough to test several of them.

## Strong Questions and Weak Questions

| Weak question | Why it is weak | Stronger version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is it a red stapler? | Almost certainly "no", so it teaches almost nothing. | Is it something found in an office? |
| Is it big or small? | Not a yes or no question. | Is it bigger than a microwave? |
| Is it a person or a place? | Two things at once, so a "yes" is ambiguous. | Is it a person? |
| Is it cool? | Subjective, so the answerer has to guess your meaning. | Is it something people use every day? |
| Is it sometimes red? | Vague about the answer you need. | Is it usually brown? |

## What to Think of When You Are the Answerer

Choosing well keeps the game fun for everyone. A few guidelines:

- **Pick something the group could reasonably know.** An obscure local shop is a bad answer. A well-known landmark is a good one.
- **Stick to the agreed category.** If you said "a thing", do not choose a person.
- **Be consistent.** Think of one specific item and keep it in mind. Switching halfway is cheating.
- **Answer honestly and quickly.** A hesitant answerer slows the game and gives away hints.
- **Adjust the difficulty to the players.** Younger players enjoy familiar objects. Experienced groups like layered subjects such as historical events or abstract ideas.

If you cannot think of anything, you can let chance help. The [Yes No Wheel](/yes-no-wheel/) lets you add your own options, such as categories or subjects, and spin for one, and the [Yes No Button](/) is useful for settling who goes first.

## 50 Ideas for What to Guess

**Easy (good for kids):** apple, dog, school bus, pizza, rainbow, bicycle, snowman, banana, teddy bear, birthday cake.

**Medium (everyday objects and places):** toaster, umbrella, lighthouse, library, backpack, guitar, kangaroo, swimming pool, mailbox, microscope.

**Hard (stretches the questions):** a shadow, a lightning bolt, the moon, a fire alarm, a passport, a rainbow trout, a traffic jam, a paper airplane, the Eiffel Tower, a parachute.

**Famous people and characters:** Albert Einstein, Cleopatra, Sherlock Holmes, Spider-Man, Mozart, Frida Kahlo, Harry Potter, Serena Williams, Leonardo da Vinci, Cinderella.

**Abstract ideas (for experts):** friendship, gravity, democracy, silence, time, a dream, a joke, an echo, luck, a deadline.

## Popular Variations

The classic version has many relatives. Here are the ones worth knowing:

- **Animal, Vegetable, or Mineral.** The oldest known version starts with the answerer announcing which of the three groups the subject belongs to. It is a good way to shrink the search space right away.
- **Person, Place, or Thing.** The same idea with friendlier categories, which is why it is common with children.
- **Category-limited rounds.** Pick a theme such as movies, food, animals, sports, or history. Everything is easier to narrow down, which helps beginners.
- **"Maybe" allowed.** Some groups let the answerer say "sometimes", "partly", or "maybe". It makes the game more forgiving for ambiguous subjects. You can model this at home with the [Yes No Maybe mode](/) as a reminder of the three allowed replies.
- **No question limit.** In Hungary the game is called Barkochba and is played without a fixed limit.
- **Timed version.** In the Philippine game Pinoy Henyo, teams guess within two minutes, and the answerer may only say "yes", "no", or "maybe".

## A Short History

Twenty Questions is far older than most people expect. The game was already common by the 1780s, and writer Hannah More described teaching it at a London dinner party in 1786. In the twentieth century it became a hit on radio: an American version premiered on February 2, 1946, and a BBC version ran from 1947 to 1976. One notable footnote is that a Polish television version in the 1960s was reportedly cancelled after two mathematicians used a binary search strategy to win. All of these details are summarized on Wikipedia's page for [Twenty questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty_questions).

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Asking about the answer too early.** A narrow guess on question two is a wasted turn.
- **Asking questions that are not yes or no.** "What color is it?" breaks the rules. Ask "Is it a warm color?" or "Is it red?" instead.
- **Combining ideas.** "Is it big and heavy?" cannot be answered cleanly when it is only one of the two.
- **Forgetting earlier answers.** Keep notes.
- **Letting the answerer pick something nobody could guess.** Agree on what is fair before you start.
- **Stopping at nineteen.** If you have a good guess, use the last question on it rather than another property.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many players do you need for 20 Questions?

You need at least two: one answerer and one guesser. The game works best with three to eight players, because more people means more questions asked in parallel and more ideas on the table. In larger groups, take turns asking so each person contributes.

### Does the final guess count as one of the twenty questions?

In most house rules, yes. A wrong guess uses up a question just like any other, so the guessers have twenty total questions including attempts to name the answer. Agree on this before the game begins, because it changes how carefully players should guess.

### What if the answer is neither yes nor no?

Many questions have messy answers, such as whether a tomato is a fruit. Either let the answerer say "sometimes" or "partly", or ask the questioner to rephrase. Agreeing to allow a "maybe" before the game starts avoids most arguments and keeps the pace up.

### What are the best first questions in 20 Questions?

The best first questions divide the possibilities as evenly as possible. Good examples are "Is it alive?", "Is it a person?", "Is it man-made?", and "Could you hold it in one hand?". Avoid naming specific objects until you have narrowed things down.

### Is 20 Questions really enough to guess anything?

Mathematically, twenty perfectly chosen yes or no questions can distinguish more than one million possibilities. In practice, you rarely get perfect halves, but a good questioner can still find most everyday objects and famous people within twenty questions.

### Can you play 20 Questions alone?

Yes. A simple solo version is to think of something and write down the twenty questions someone else would need, or to play against a computer or friend by message. Many people also use the structure to practice narrowing down real problems, which is a useful skill.
