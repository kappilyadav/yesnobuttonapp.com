You wrote two columns. One has seven items, the other has six, and you feel exactly as stuck as before. Or worse, the longer column points one way and your stomach points the other.

That is a common experience, and it rarely means you are bad at deciding. It means the list was built in a way that makes it hard for it to help. The pros and cons list is one of the oldest decision tools we have, but the version most people use is a stripped-down copy of the original, and the parts that got dropped are the parts that did the work.

This guide covers where the method came from, the seven most common ways a list quietly fails, and a short procedure that fixes each one. It also covers the cases where a list is the wrong tool entirely.

## The Short Answer

- **A list works when it forces you to weigh reasons, not count them.** The original method did exactly that.
- **Most failures are process failures:** counting lines, listing features instead of consequences, writing the list after you have already decided, and leaving out options.
- **Precise weights matter less than a complete, honest set of criteria.** Research on simple scoring models suggests that crude weights perform about as well as fancy ones.
- **Deal-breakers do not belong in a column.** A risk you cannot recover from should be checked first, separately from the tally.
- **Some decisions are not list-shaped.** If the things that matter most are hard to put into words, a list will quietly overrepresent whatever is easy to write down.

## What Franklin Actually Did

The pros and cons list is usually traced to a 1772 letter from Benjamin Franklin to the scientist Joseph Priestley, who was trying to decide whether to leave his post as a minister in Leeds for a position with the Earl of Shelburne. Franklin declined to tell him what to choose and described how he handled his own hard decisions. The letter is reproduced at [Farnam Street](https://www.fs.blog/2012/09/benjamin-franklins-rule-for-decision-making/) and in other collections.

Three details of his method are rarely repeated:

1. **It took days.** He wrote the two columns, headed Pro and Con, and spent "three or four Days Consideration," adding short notes as reasons occurred to him. It was not a one-sitting brainstorm.
2. **Reasons were cancelled, not counted.** In his words, "If I find a Reason pro equal to some two Reasons con, I strike out the three." So one strong reason could outweigh two weaker ones on the other side. That is weighting, done by hand.
3. **He knew it was approximate.** He called the method "Moral or Prudential Algebra" and admitted the weights could not be measured with the precision of algebra. His claim was only that looking at reasons side by side helped him judge better and act less rashly.

Compare that with the typical modern list, written in ten minutes, tallied by line count, with every bullet implicitly worth the same. The format is the same. The method is not.

## Seven Ways a Pros and Cons List Fails

### 1. You counted lines instead of weighing them

Seven pros and six cons does not mean "pros win." One of the six cons might be "my partner would be miserable" and three of the seven pros might be variations of "it sounds fun." Lines are not units of value. If you do nothing else, do what Franklin did and cancel items against each other by importance rather than tallying them.

### 2. You wrote the list after you had already decided

This is the most common one, and it is hard to spot from the inside. If you already lean toward an option, reasons for it come easily and reasons against it feel nitpicky. The list becomes a document that justifies a conclusion rather than a test of it.

A quick check: finish the tally, then notice your reaction when it points one way. A small sinking feeling when option A wins is information. It does not mean the list is wrong, but it often means you are weighing something that is not on it. Name that thing and add it. (This is a practical heuristic, not a research finding, and it works best when you are honest about the reaction.)

### 3. One side got more effort

It is easier to list pros for the exciting option and cons for the scary one. Both lists end up long, but each is long on the side that confirms what you already feel. Fix this with a rule: give each option the same time (say, five minutes each) and write at least four items for both its upsides and its downsides before you compare.

### 4. You listed features, not consequences

"Has a gym" is a feature. "I would use it twice a week and save about $40 a month" is a consequence. "Bigger title" is a feature; "more meetings and less hands-on work, which I said I hate" is a consequence. A list of features feels productive but leaves out the only question that matters: what will actually change in my life? For each item, finish the sentence "which means that…".

### 5. You treated unlikely and likely outcomes the same

"Might get promoted" and "will definitely have a longer commute" both look like one bullet. Add a rough likelihood next to anything uncertain: almost certain, likely, possible, long shot. A long shot with a big payoff can still matter, but it should not be allowed to sit in the same column with the same visual weight as a certainty.

### 6. A deal-breaker is hiding in the cons column

If an item is "I could not recover from this" (an unaffordable payment, a legal risk, a health risk, a serious harm to someone you love), then it is not a con to be traded off against pros. It is a filter. Check for these first, on a separate line, before you start the tally. If an option fails the filter, it is out, no matter how long its pro column is.

### 7. The decision was never really a two-option choice

Pros and cons lists nudge you into "A or B." Many real decisions have a third path: do it later, do a smaller version, negotiate the terms, or try it for a month. Before you make the list, ask: is there a version of this that keeps most of the upside with less of the downside? Adding that option often ends the deadlock more effectively than any amount of weighing.

## A Better Method in Seven Steps

You do not need an app or template. A piece of paper or a notes file is enough.

1. **Write the decision as a question with two or three real options.** Include a "smaller or later" option if one exists.
2. **Run the filter first.** For each option, ask whether it breaks a hard limit: money you cannot lose, a promise you will not break, a health or safety line. Cross out options that fail.
3. **List criteria, not reasons.** Write 4 to 7 things that matter, such as cost, time, stress, growth, and relationships. These are the things you are really trading off. Do this before you look at the options in detail, so the criteria are not shaped by the option you like.
4. **Weight the criteria from 1 to 3.** A 3 means "I would be upset if this went badly." A 1 means "nice to have." Resist spreading everything across the scale. Most lists should have only one or two 3s.
5. **Score each option 1 to 5 on each criterion,** using consequences ("which means that…"), not features.
6. **Multiply and add,** then look at the margin, not just the winner.
7. **Run a feelings check** (see below), sleep on it, and revisit once if the margin is small.

### A worked example

Say you are choosing between two apartments. You decide that rent and commute matter most (weight 3), light and quiet matter somewhat (2), landlord reputation matters somewhat (2), and lease flexibility is minor (1).

| Criterion (weight) | Apartment A | Apartment B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rent (3) | 5 | 3 |
| Commute (3) | 2 | 5 |
| Light and quiet (2) | 5 | 4 |
| Landlord (2) | 2 | 4 |
| Flexibility (1) | 4 | 2 |
| **Unweighted total** | **18** | **18** |
| **Weighted total** | **39** | **42** |

Unweighted, it is a tie, so a plain count of pros and cons would leave you stuck. With weights, B edges ahead by three points out of a possible 55. That is a small margin, and the honest conclusion is "these are close, and B is slightly better on the things I said matter most." It is not "B is the answer." A close margin is a signal to move on to the feelings check and, if you still cannot tell, to treat the choice as a near-tie (see [How to Decide Between Two Equally Good Options](/blog/how-to-decide-between-two-equally-good-options/)).

## Do the Weights Even Matter?

Fair question. Weighting adds effort and a false sense of precision, so it is worth knowing how much it buys you.

Psychologist Robyn Dawes made a well-known argument in 1979 in [The Robust Beauty of Improper Linear Models in Decision Making](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/dawes/the-robust-beauty-of-improper-linear-models-in-decision-making.pdf) that simple models with equal or crude weights often predict about as well as carefully fitted regression models, and better than unaided intuition in the settings he examined. The reasons are that fitted weights partly capture the noise in the sample, while simple weights are not misled by it.

What that does and does not mean for you:

- **It supports scoring over gut-only choices** when you can name the factors that matter.
- **It suggests you should not agonise over whether rent is a 3 or a 2.5.** Crude weights are fine.
- **It also hints that the real work is picking the right criteria.** Leaving out an important factor costs more than getting a weight slightly wrong.
- **It does not prove a score beats judgment on personal, one-off decisions.** Those studies are mostly about prediction tasks with measurable outcomes. Treat your scores as a structured way of thinking, not as an oracle.

## The Feelings Check

Once you have the numbers, look at how you feel about the result. There are three common outcomes:

- **Relief.** The list matches what you wanted. Good. You have a decision and a record of why.
- **Disappointment.** The list points one way, but you wanted the other. Ask what is missing from the criteria. Often it is something you were embarrassed to write down, like prestige, fear of what someone will think, or loyalty to a plan you made years ago. Add it, then re-score honestly. You may still go with the list. The point is that you now know what you were weighing.
- **Nothing.** You feel indifferent. That usually means the options are close in practice, and you can stop optimising.

If you want a gentler version of the same test, assign each option to one side of a random tool, ask it, and notice your reaction to the answer before you act on it. ## When Not to Use a List

- **When the options are genuinely equal.** More analysis will not help. Pick, or use a tie-breaker.
- **When the main factors are hard to put in words.** A list favors what is easy to name (salary, distance) over what is hard to name (belonging, meaning). If a list keeps feeling beside the point, that may be why.
- **When the decision is emotionally loaded and you are in the middle of the emotion.** Come back after a night's sleep. Franklin's multi-day timeline had a point.
- **When you keep rewriting the list.** If you have made it three times, the list is not the problem. Read [Analysis Paralysis: 5 Reasons You Can't Decide](/blog/analysis-paralysis-why-you-cant-decide/) for what to do instead.
- **When the stakes are medical, legal, or financial.** A list can help you organise your questions, but it should not replace professional advice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why doesn't my pros and cons list help me decide?

Usually because it counts items rather than weighing them, lists features rather than consequences, or was written to support a choice you had already made. Fix it by choosing criteria first, weighting them 1 to 3, scoring each option on consequences, and checking for deal-breakers separately.

### Who invented the pros and cons list?

The best-known early description is Benjamin Franklin's 1772 letter to Joseph Priestley, in which he described a two-column method he called "Moral or Prudential Algebra." Whether it was the very first such list is hard to prove, since similar ideas were probably in use before. What is documented is his specific method: columns, several days of additions, and cancelling reasons of equal weight.

### Should I weight the items on a pros and cons list?

Yes, at least roughly. A 1 to 3 scale is enough. Crude weights tend to perform about as well as precise ones in studies of simple scoring models, so do not spend long on them. Spend your effort on choosing criteria that cover what matters.

### What if the pros and cons are equal?

A near-tie means both options are probably acceptable and the cost of choosing wrongly is small. Run the feelings check, and if you still feel nothing, pick using a tie-breaker, such as a coin flip, a random tool, or the option that is easier to reverse.

### What should I do when my gut disagrees with the list?

Treat the disagreement as information. Ask what your gut is responding to that is not in your criteria, add it, and re-score. Either the list changes or you understand your gut better. Both are useful outcomes.

### Can a pros and cons list be wrong?

Yes. It can only reflect what you put in it. If you leave out an important factor, score an option you favor generously, or ignore a risk, the arithmetic will be tidy and the result misleading. That is why the filter, the equal-effort rule, and the feelings check are worth doing.
