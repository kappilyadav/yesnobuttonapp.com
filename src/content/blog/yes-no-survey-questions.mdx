Yes or no questions look like the easiest thing in a survey: quick to write, quick to answer, quick to count. They are also one of the easiest ways to collect misleading data. Used in the right place, a yes or no question gives you a clean, unambiguous answer. Used in the wrong place, it hides the very detail you are trying to find.

This guide explains what a yes or no (dichotomous) survey question is, when it is the right tool, the biases to watch for, how to rewrite weak questions, and how many responses you actually need to trust the results.

## What Is a Yes or No Survey Question?

A yes or no question, called a dichotomous question in research, offers exactly two answers. Typical examples are "Have you purchased from us before?" or "Do you own a car?". The two options are mutually exclusive, so every respondent must fall into one group or the other.

That simplicity is the main appeal. A yes or no question is easy to understand, takes a second to answer on a phone, and produces data that is simple to count. Those same qualities are also its biggest limitation.

## Pros and Cons at a Glance

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Very fast to answer, which helps completion rates. | Forces a choice even when the real answer is "it depends". |
| Easy to analyze: just a percentage. | Cannot show how strongly someone feels. |
| Clear and unambiguous for factual questions. | Lumps very different people into one group. |
| Works well on mobile and for screening. | Prone to acquiescence bias when phrased as agreement. |
| Great for branching to follow-up questions. | Poor for opinions, attitudes, and satisfaction. |

## When Yes or No Questions Work Best

Dichotomous questions are at their best when the true answer really is binary and the respondent knows it.

- **Screening and eligibility.** "Are you 18 or older?" or "Do you currently work in healthcare?" A person either qualifies or does not.
- **Factual events.** "Did you attend the event?" "Have you used the product in the last 30 days?" Simple facts have simple answers.
- **Branching logic.** A yes can open a follow-up. "Did you contact customer support?" leads only the people who said yes to "How satisfied were you with the response?".
- **Consent and agreement to terms.** A clear yes or no is exactly what you need.
- **Quick polls.** For a one-question pulse check, a yes or no keeps friction as low as possible.
- **Low-effort contexts.** On a phone, in an email, or in a kiosk, shorter surveys get more responses.

## When to Avoid Them

Yes or no questions are a poor fit when you need nuance.

- **Opinions and attitudes.** "Are you satisfied with our service?" forces a gray area into a hard line. A five-point scale shows whether people are slightly or extremely satisfied.
- **Frequency or intensity.** "Do you use our product?" hides the difference between someone who tried it once and someone who uses it daily. SurveyMethods makes this point with a usage example, arguing that graded options such as "never used it", "used it but not regularly", and "use it regularly" give a truer picture. See their article on [why to avoid dichotomous questions](https://surveymethods.com/why-to-avoid-dichotomous-questions/).
- **Sensitive topics.** People may answer in the way they think is expected, or skip the question entirely.
- **Complex ideas.** If a question needs a sentence of explanation, it probably needs more than two choices.

## The Five Most Common Problems (and How to Fix Them)

### 1. Leading wording

Small changes in wording can move results a lot. Pew Research Center reports that in 2003, 68% of Americans favored military action in Iraq to end Saddam Hussein's rule, but when the question added "even if it meant that U.S. forces might suffer thousands of casualties", support fell to 43%. Both are yes or no questions. The only difference is the framing. You can read more in Pew's guide to [writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/our-methods/u-s-surveys/writing-survey-questions/).

**Fix:** Use neutral language. Avoid words that carry emotion or suggest the "right" answer.

### 2. Double-barreled questions

"Are you happy with our prices and customer service?" asks about two things. If someone loves the service but hates the price, there is no honest answer. Pew gives a similar example about confidence in a president to handle domestic and foreign policy, and recommends asking each topic separately.

**Fix:** One idea per question. Split "and" questions into two.

### 3. Acquiescence bias

Some respondents tend to agree with whatever is put in front of them, regardless of what they believe. This is called [acquiescence bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acquiescence_bias). Pew notes that the tendency is stronger among less educated and less informed respondents and when an interviewer is present, and it recommends offering a choice between competing statements rather than agree-or-disagree wording.

**Fix:** Instead of "Do you agree that remote work is better?" ask "Which do you prefer: working remotely or working in an office?".

### 4. Negative or confusing phrasing

"Wouldn't you agree that the checkout is not difficult?" stacks negatives, and respondents cannot tell whether "yes" means agreement or disagreement.

**Fix:** State the question plainly and positively: "Was the checkout easy to use?".

### 5. Order effects

Earlier questions shape the answers to later ones. In a 2008 Pew example, 88% said they were dissatisfied with national conditions when the question came after a presidential approval question, compared with 78% when it did not. The same yes or no item can produce different numbers depending on what comes before it.

**Fix:** Group questions by topic, start with easy and engaging items, and keep the order the same if you plan to compare results over time.

## Rewrite Examples

| Weak question | Problem | Better version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do you love our amazing new design? | Leading and emotional. | Have you seen our new design? (Yes/No), then: How would you rate it? (1 to 5) |
| Are you satisfied with our products and support? | Double-barreled. | Two questions: one for products, one for support. |
| Do you ever use our app? | "Ever" is too loose to be useful. | Have you used our app in the last 30 days? |
| Don't you think the price is fair? | Negative and leading. | Is the price of this product fair? Yes, No, or Not sure |
| Do you exercise? | Too broad. | In the past week, did you exercise for at least 20 minutes on any day? |

## Add a "Not Sure" Option Where It Is Honest

A forced yes or no can produce guesses. If a respondent might truly not know or the question might not apply, consider adding "Not sure" or "Not applicable". SurveyMonkey discusses when a [not applicable answer choice](https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/not-applicable-answer-choice/) helps and when it just adds noise. As a rule of thumb, include it when a meaningful share of your audience could be in that group, and leave it out for questions every respondent can answer.

## Alternatives to Yes or No

| If you need to know... | Consider |
| --- | --- |
| How strongly someone agrees | A five-point Likert scale (strongly disagree to strongly agree) |
| Which of two options someone prefers | A forced-choice question with two statements |
| How often something happens | A frequency scale such as never, rarely, sometimes, often, always |
| Whether something applies, with uncertainty | Yes, No, and Not sure |
| What a person actually thinks | An open-ended follow-up |

A useful pattern is to pair the two: ask a quick yes or no to find out whether the topic applies, then ask a scale question only of those people. For example, "Did you contact support? (Yes/No)" followed by "How satisfied were you? (1 to 5)".

## How Many Responses Do You Need?

A yes or no question gives you a percentage, and the more people answer, the narrower the uncertainty around that percentage. For a simple random sample at a 95% confidence level, the worst-case margin of error is about 1.96 times the square root of 0.25 divided by n. That works out as:

| Responses (n) | Margin of error (95% confidence) |
| --- | --- |
| 100 | about ±9.8 points |
| 400 | about ±4.9 points |
| 1,000 | about ±3.1 points |
| 2,500 | about ±2.0 points |

So if 60% of 400 respondents say yes, the true share is likely somewhere between about 55% and 65%. To get to a margin of about ±5 points you need roughly 385 responses. Two cautions apply. First, this assumes a random sample, and an opt-in poll of your own newsletter readers is not random. Second, the margin of error does not cover bad wording, which can shift answers by far more than sampling error. To learn more, see the Wikipedia overview of the [margin of error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margin_of_error).

## A Checklist Before You Publish

- Does each question ask about exactly one thing?
- Is the wording neutral, with no emotional or loaded words?
- Is the question stated positively, without double negatives?
- Is the time frame clear, such as "in the past 30 days"?
- Would every respondent be able to answer honestly with only yes or no?
- If not, should the answer choices include "Not sure" or "Not applicable", or be a scale instead?
- Have you pretested the survey with five to ten people and asked what each question meant to them?
- Is the question order the same for everyone?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a dichotomous question?

A dichotomous question is a survey question with only two possible answers, most often yes or no. Examples include true or false and agree or disagree. It is quick to answer and easy to analyze, but it cannot capture degrees of opinion or uncertainty.

### When should I use yes or no questions in a survey?

Use them for factual, binary matters: screening questions, simple events, consent, and branching to follow-up questions. Avoid them for opinions, satisfaction, and anything that varies by degree, where a rating scale gives more useful data.

### Are yes or no questions leading questions?

Not by themselves. Any question can be leading if the wording hints at the preferred answer. Yes or no questions are especially prone to acquiescence bias when phrased as statements to agree with, so keep the wording neutral.

### What is the difference between a yes or no question and a Likert scale?

A yes or no question has two options and shows only whether something applies. A Likert scale, typically five or seven points, shows how strongly a respondent agrees or disagrees, so it captures intensity as well as direction.

### Should I include a "not sure" option?

Include it when a meaningful share of respondents might genuinely not know or the question might not apply to them. Skip it for questions everybody can answer, because an unnecessary option can pull people away from a real answer.

### How many people need to answer a yes or no survey?

It depends on how precise you want to be. For a random sample at 95% confidence, about 385 responses give a margin of error near plus or minus 5 points, and about 1,000 responses give roughly plus or minus 3 points.
