You clicked "place order," or signed the paperwork, or walked out of the store with the bag, and about an hour later your stomach dropped. *Did I really need that? Was it too much? Should I have waited?*

That feeling has a name, buyer's remorse, and nearly everyone who has bought anything meaningful has had it. Most advice on the subject stops at "it is normal, give it time." That is true but not much help when your return window closes in nine days and you have to do something.

The useful fact is that buyer's remorse is not one problem. It is at least four, and they need different responses. There is also a trap built into the timing: the feeling is strongest right when it is least reliable, and your attachment to the item grows right when you most need to be honest about whether it was a mistake. This guide covers how to tell the types apart, why the feeling misleads, and a five-question test for deciding whether to keep or return what you bought.

## The Short Answer

- **Find the return deadline and the conditions first.** Everything else is easier once you know how long you have and what returning would cost you.
- **Work out which type of remorse you have.** Money stress, finding something better, a bad fit, and plain "closing the door" jitters call for different actions.
- **Do not judge the purchase by how you feel in the first day.** Early remorse often fades by itself, so it is weak evidence that you made a mistake.
- **Do not judge it by how attached you feel at the end of the window either.** Owning something makes you value it more, which is also a poor guide.
- **Judge it on evidence:** can you afford it, would you buy it again today as a stranger to it, and did using it prove its value?

## Buyer's Remorse Is Four Different Problems

"I regret buying this" can mean very different things. Name yours before you decide anything.

| Type | What it sounds like | What it really is | Usual best move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Money stress** | "I can't really afford this." | The purchase puts pressure on rent, debt, or savings. | Return it. This is the one type where waiting rarely helps. |
| **Comparison regret** | "I just saw it cheaper / a better one exists." | A new option appeared after you chose. | Check for price adjustment or a swap, and weigh the saving against the hassle. |
| **Fit regret** | "It isn't what I expected." | The item does not do what you needed. | Test it against your original reason for buying. Return it if it fails. |
| **Closing-the-door jitters** | "What if I'd waited? What if there was something better?" | Nothing is wrong with the item. You lost your other options. | Wait and use it. This type often fades without any action. |

Most people lump these together and either return everything in a panic or keep everything out of stubbornness. The first step toward a calm decision is to ask which of the four you are actually feeling. Many people have more than one at once, such as jitters about a purchase that also happens to be a stretch financially. In that case, the money question comes first because it is the only one with a real cost if you get it wrong.

## Why the Feeling Shows Up

### You just gave up your other options

Before you choose, several options stay open and each looks appealing. After you choose, all but one disappear. Psychologists call the discomfort of that shift **cognitive dissonance**, and one classic finding is that after a choice, people tend to rate the chosen option higher and the rejected one lower, as if to justify the decision.

A 2007 study by Egan, Santos and Bloom in *Psychological Science* found [a similar pattern in preschool children and in capuchin monkeys](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/cognitive-dissonance-observed-children-and-monkeys): after choosing between two options they had rated about equally, they tended to prefer a new option over the one they had rejected. The study has drawn criticism about its design, so treat the exact size of the effect as uncertain. The broad idea, that choosing changes how we value what we chose and what we left behind, is well supported across decades of work.

This matters here because remorse and justification are two sides of the same adjustment. Some people feel the discomfort first and then settle as their mind reconciles the choice. Others feel remorse that does not settle, because the purchase has a real problem. The challenge is telling them apart.

### Some people are more exposed than others

In a 2002 paper in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Barry Schwartz and colleagues built a scale to measure whether people tend to **maximize** (look for the best possible option) or **satisfice** (accept one that is good enough). Across seven samples, they reported that [higher maximizing scores went with lower happiness and more regret](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf), and that maximizers were less satisfied with consumer decisions. Later work has questioned how well the scale measures maximizing and how to interpret the link, so it is better read as a tendency than a verdict on a personality type.

The practical point is simple. If your standard is "the best one available," the existence of any better option anywhere turns into a reason for remorse. If your standard is "good enough for my needs," it does not. We cover this in more detail in [Analysis Paralysis: 5 Reasons You Can't Decide](/blog/analysis-paralysis-why-you-cant-decide/).

### An easy return policy changes how you feel

Two lines of research point in the same direction. First, in a series of studies, Gilbert and Ebert (2002) found that when people could change their minds, they ended up [liking their choice less than people who could not](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/decisions-and-revisions-the-affective-forecasting-of-changeable-outcomes-4800), even though most participants said they would want the option to change. In short, keeping the exit open interferes with the process that normally helps you feel good about a choice.

Second, Stacy Wood's 2001 research in the *Journal of Marketing Research* on remote purchasing, as summarized by other sources, suggests that lenient return policies lead buyers to treat an order as a trial, and that they may still become more attached to the product by the time it arrives.

Together these suggest something you can use. A generous return window gives you room to test the product, which is valuable. But the window is also where doubt can linger. Treat it as a testing period with a clear end, not an open door you can keep revisiting.

## The Trap: Two Biases Pulling in Opposite Directions

Here is why you cannot just "go with how you feel."

- **At hour one**, remorse pushes you toward returning. The feeling is partly dissonance and partly the loss of your other options. It is loud, but it often says nothing about the product itself.
- **At day 25**, the **endowment effect** pushes you toward keeping. Once you own something, you tend to value it more than you would have before. It has your fingerprints on it and it seems like part of your life. That feeling is no more reliable than the first.

A good decision process has to avoid both. That means replacing feelings with things you can check: what it costs, whether it did its job, and whether you would choose it again from a neutral starting point.

## The Five-Question Keep-or-Return Test

Do this once, ideally on day two or three, and then again at the end of your window if you are still unsure. Write the answers down. It takes about ten minutes.

1. **Deadline and conditions.** What is the last day to return it? Is there a restocking fee, an opened-item rule, or a requirement to keep the packaging? Put the date in your calendar before you do anything else. If you do not know the answer, find out now.
2. **The money check.** If you had to pay for this again from this month's money, would it cause trouble with bills, debt, or savings goals? If yes, return it. A purchase that causes financial strain is not fixed by waiting.
3. **The stranger test.** Imagine you do not own this. It is on a shelf, it costs what you paid, and you have to buy it today. Would you? Be honest about the effort of returning it, and leave that out of the question. This counters the endowment effect, because it removes the "it's mine" feeling.
4. **The use test.** Did you use it in the way you imagined when you bought it? Set a goal when you buy: "I will use this three times in two weeks" or "I will wear it to one event." If you could not meet your own target during the return window, that is meaningful.
5. **The regret-later test.** If you return it, what is the realistic chance you will want it back? If you keep it, what is the realistic chance you will resent it in six months? Pick the option whose bad case is easier to live with.

**How to read your answers.**

- A *no* on question 2 means return it.
- If you answered *no* to question 3 and *no* to question 4, the purchase probably missed its purpose. Return it.
- If you answered *yes* to both 3 and 4, and money is not an issue, the remorse was likely closing-the-door jitters. Keep it, and consider writing "kept it on [date], because [reason]" so you can refer back to it.
- If the answers are mixed, you are probably facing a true toss-up. See [How to Decide Between Two Equally Good Options](/blog/how-to-decide-between-two-equally-good-options/) for how to break a real tie. Often, keeping is the right default if the cost of being wrong is small.

## A Timeline for the Return Window

| When | What to do | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **First 24 hours** | Find the deadline and conditions. Take no other action unless the money check clearly fails. | Returning on impulse, or hunting for evidence that you made the right choice |
| **Days 2 to 7** | Use the item the way you planned. Run the five-question test once. Check your own price comparisons without obsessing over them. | Reading endless reviews. After you have bought, more reviews tend to feed either reassurance-seeking or new worries. Your own use of the item is better evidence. |
| **Last 3 days** | Run the test again. Decide, then act the same day, because delay is how windows close. | Letting the window lapse because you are "still thinking" |
| **After the window** | If you kept it, count the decision as closed and write down your reason. | Re-opening the decision every time you see a sale price |

## Bigger Purchases: Cars, Homes, and Contracts

Large purchases need extra care because the usual "I'll just return it" assumption often does not hold.

- **Cars.** It is a widespread belief that you get three days to cancel a car purchase. In general you do not. The FTC's Cooling-Off Rule, which gives a three-business-day right to cancel for certain sales, mainly covers purchases made at your home or at a seller's temporary location, and it carves out some vehicle sales. A purchase on a dealer's lot is generally not covered. Some states have their own rules or dealer-offered return programs, so check your contract and your state's consumer protection office before assuming you have a right to back out.
- **Homes.** Your real cooling-off period is the time before closing. Inspection, financing, and appraisal contingencies exist for this reason. If you feel remorse after the offer is accepted, check what your contract lets you do, and talk to your agent or attorney right away, because deadlines in these contracts can be short.
- **Door-to-door and similar sales.** If you signed a contract at home or at a temporary sales venue, the Cooling-Off Rule may give you a short window to cancel. The [text of the rule is public](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-429), and exemptions and dollar thresholds apply, so read it or ask a consumer protection agency.
- **Online orders.** Return rules depend on the store, not on a federal "change of mind" right. Read the store's policy, and if it is unclear, ask before you buy.

None of this is legal advice. For a large purchase where you may want out, contact a consumer protection agency or an attorney before the clock runs out.

## If You Can't Return It

Sometimes the window has passed or the item is final sale. You still have options.

- **Count the real cost accurately.** Sunk cost is gone whether you keep it or sell it. The only question is what is best from here: use it, resell it, or give it away.
- **Make it earn its place.** Divide the price by the number of times you expect to use it. A jacket that costs $200 and gets worn 100 times costs $2 per wear. That framing helps with expensive things that are good, and it quickly exposes the ones that are not going to be used.
- **Resell sooner rather than later.** Many items lose value fast. If you are sure you will not use it, selling it now usually recovers more than waiting.
- **Extract the lesson, not the guilt.** Which type of remorse did you have? If it was money stress, the fix is a spending limit. If it was fit, the fix is a trial period before big buys. If it was comparison, set a rule for when to stop researching.

## How to Prevent Buyer's Remorse Next Time

1. **Write the keep criteria at purchase.** "I will keep this if I use it three times in two weeks." You are less likely to be swayed later by either dissonance or attachment.
2. **Use a waiting list for non-urgent buys.** Put it on a list for 48 hours (small items) or 30 days (large ones). If you still want it, buy it. Many wants fade without a decision at all.
3. **Pick your standard before you shop.** Decide what "good enough" means, such as three must-have features and a price limit. Once something meets the standard, stop comparing.
4. **Check the return policy before checkout.** Not after.
5. **Do not shop when you feel low, rushed, or pressured.** If a seller says it is "now or never," ask yourself why it can't wait until tomorrow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is buyer's remorse?

It is the regret, anxiety, or doubt you feel after making a purchase. It is not one thing. It can come from money stress, from finding a better or cheaper option, from a product that does not fit your needs, or from the discomfort of giving up your other choices after you commit.

### How long does buyer's remorse last?

There is no single figure. Remorse based on the discomfort of having chosen often eases within days as you use the item and settle in. Remorse based on a real problem, such as affordability or poor fit, does not fade, because the cause is still there. That is why the test above focuses on the cause, not the feeling.

### Is buyer's remorse a sign I made a mistake?

Not necessarily. Early remorse is common even with good purchases because choosing involves giving up alternatives. Treat it as a prompt to check the facts. If you can afford it, would buy it again today, and have used it for its purpose, the feeling is probably not telling you much.

### Should I return something I regret buying right away?

Only if the money check fails or you already know the item is unsuitable. Otherwise, find your deadline, use the item for a few days, and run the five-question test. Impulsive returns driven by a first-day feeling are as unreliable as impulsive purchases.

### Do I get three days to cancel a car purchase?

Generally no. The FTC's Cooling-Off Rule mainly covers sales made at your home or other locations away from the seller's permanent place of business, and a purchase on a dealer's lot is usually not covered. Some states and dealers offer their own return options, so check your contract and local rules before you sign.

### How do I stop second-guessing a purchase after the return window ends?

Write down why you bought it and why you chose to keep it, then treat the matter as closed. Revisit only if something new and relevant appears, not because you saw it for less. Our guide on [how to stop second-guessing yourself](/blog/how-to-stop-second-guessing-yourself/) covers this in more detail.

### Can the Yes No Button decide whether I keep or return something?

It can be a useful mirror for small purchases once you have run the test. Ask the question, see your gut reaction to the answer, and note whether you feel relief or disappointment. It should not replace the money check, and it is not a good idea for large purchases with real financial risk.
