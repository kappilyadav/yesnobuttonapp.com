You made the decision. You sent the email, accepted the offer, picked the answer, ended the conversation. And now your mind will not leave it alone. *Was that right? What if I'd said the other thing? Should I change it?*

The standard advice is "trust your gut" or "stop overthinking." That advice has two problems. It does not tell you how, and it is sometimes wrong. A well-known body of research on changing answers on tests shows that second-guessing is, on average, *not* the mistake most people think it is. The real problem is a specific kind of second-guessing: reopening a decision when nothing new has happened.

This guide separates the two kinds, explains why some decisions keep nagging at you, and gives you a five-step "doubt audit" that tells you whether to review a decision or close it. It is written for everyday decisions at work, in relationships, and in life. If your doubt feels constant, painful, or hard to control, see the note near the end.

## The Short Answer

- **Ask one question first: has anything new happened since I decided?** If yes, review the decision once. If no, you are replaying, not reviewing.
- **"Always go with your first instinct" is mostly a myth.** In test-taking research, changing an answer more often helped than hurt. The mistake is changing without a reason.
- **Keeping the door open makes doubt worse.** Research suggests that being able to change your mind reduces how happy you are with a choice. Close the decision on purpose.
- **Decide your revisit rules in advance.** Write down what would change your mind and when you will look again. Then you do not have to keep deciding all day.
- **Match your effort to the type of door.** Reversible decisions deserve speed, and irreversible ones deserve care before the decision, not after.

## Two Kinds of Second-Guessing

Not all doubt is the same. Compare these two situations.

**Checking.** You accepted a job offer, and the next day the company announces layoffs in your department. You look again. This is second-guessing based on new information, and it is rational.

**Replaying.** You accepted a job offer, and two days later you have no new facts. But at 2 a.m. you imagine the other offer, rerun the salary comparison, and picture saying no instead. This is second-guessing without new information, and it produces discomfort without producing answers.

The difference is not how strong the doubt feels. Replaying can feel urgent and convincing. The difference is **whether anything has changed that you can point to**. A quick way to tell:

| | Checking | Replaying |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trigger** | A new fact, deadline, or event | A mood, a quiet moment, or seeing someone else's choice |
| **Content** | "The situation changed because…" | "What if I had…" |
| **Ends when** | You review and reach a verdict | You get distracted, and it returns later |
| **Output** | A decision to keep, adjust, or reverse | More doubt |
| **Right response** | Review once, with a time limit | Close it, and handle the feeling |

## What the Evidence Says

### "Stick with your first answer" is a myth

Many students are told to avoid changing answers on multiple-choice tests. In 2005, Justin Kruger, Derrick Wirtz and Dale Miller examined this in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. According to [an APA Monitor report](https://www.apa.org/monitor/apr05/instincts) on the work, in 1,561 introductory psychology exams, about 51% of answer changes went from wrong to right while about 25% went from right to wrong. The rest were wrong-to-wrong changes or other cases.

Why do people still believe the opposite? Kruger and colleagues argued, in [the paper's abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15898871/), that changing away from a correct answer produces more self-blame than keeping a wrong one, so those errors stick in memory. The "if only I'd stayed with my first answer" memories are vivid, and the many times a change helped are not. That uneven memory feels like proof.

A later study by Couchman and colleagues (2016) added a refinement. Students rated their confidence for each question, and the researchers found that confidence was a good guide to when changing or keeping was wise. According to [a summary of the work](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2022/2/24-1) and the authors' own write-up, on the questions students were least sure about, they were wrong more than half the time when they kept their first answer.

### What this does and doesn't mean for your life

Be careful here. A multiple-choice test has a correct answer, and your life decision probably does not. So the lesson is not "always change your mind." It is narrower and more useful:

- **Your memory of regrets is biased.** You will remember the one time you changed a good decision more vividly than the many times a second look helped.
- **Revising is not the enemy.** Reconsidering can be the right move.
- **What matters is the signal behind the change.** In the exam studies, the useful signal was low confidence combined with a reason. In life, the equivalent is new information or a specific flaw you can name.

This is why the five-step audit below asks for a reason, not a feeling.

### An open door keeps the doubt alive

In 2002, Daniel Gilbert and Jane Ebert published [a series of studies](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/decisions-and-revisions-the-affective-forecasting-of-changeable-outcomes-4800) in which participants who could change their minds about a choice ended up liking it less than those who could not. Most participants still said they wanted the option to change. The authors suggested that people do not foresee that irreversibility can leave them happier.

That fits a common experience: a decision you cannot undo tends to stop nagging sooner than one you can. If you can still reverse a choice, your mind has an excuse to keep working on it. That points to a practical rule: **a decision you plan to keep needs a moment where you close it.** More on that below.

### Maximizing makes it worse

Barry Schwartz and colleagues reported in 2002 that people who score higher on a measure of **maximizing**, meaning seeking the very best option, [tend to report lower happiness and more regret](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf). Later critics have questioned the measurement, so take it as a tendency. The practical takeaway is that if your standard is "the best possible choice," you will keep finding reasons to doubt any real one. A standard of "good enough, given what I knew" is one you can actually meet.

## The Doubt Audit: Five Steps, About Ten Minutes

Use this when a decision is nagging you. You need a pen or a notes app.

**1. Name the doubt in one sentence.**
Vague doubt ("something feels off") is hard to resolve. Specific doubt is easier: "I'm worried the new job has a worse manager than I assumed." If you cannot say it in one sentence, write down "I feel uneasy and can't say why." That is also useful information, and it points to step 5.

**2. Run the new-information test.**
Ask: *Is there any fact I did not have when I decided?* A fact is something you could show another person: an email, a number, an event, a conversation. A thought, a mood, or a "what if" is not a fact.

- **If yes, go to step 3.**
- **If no, go to step 5.**

**3. Check the fact against your original reasons.**
Look at why you decided. Does the new information break one of those reasons? Or does it only add a small worry? Rate it: *breaks a reason*, *changes the odds a bit*, or *noise*.

**4. Give yourself a verdict, once.**
Choose one of three:

- **Hold.** The decision stands.
- **Adjust.** Keep the decision but change how you carry it out (add a condition, request a delay, ask a question).
- **Reverse.** Only if the new information breaks a core reason.

Put a time limit on this, such as 15 minutes, and write the verdict down. Then the review is finished. A review that never ends is just rumination.

**5. Close it, even if the feeling remains.**
If there is no new information, your task is not to solve the doubt. It is to stop treating it as a question. Write a sentence you can repeat: "I decided this on [date] with the information I had, for [reason]. Nothing new has happened. The decision stands." The feeling may stay for a while. That is okay. A feeling can be present without needing a response.

## Use a Decision Log

A decision log is a short note you write at the moment you decide. It takes two minutes and it prevents most of the later replaying.

> **Decision:** Accept the offer at the smaller company.
> **Date:** 12 March
> **Why:** More ownership of my work; salary is within 5% of the other offer; shorter commute.
> **Biggest risk I accepted:** Less job security.
> **What would make me revisit:** Two missed paydays, or a major change in my role in the first 90 days.
> **Next review:** 12 June.

Why it helps:

- **It fights hindsight bias.** When things go slightly wrong, it feels as if you "should have known." Your log shows what you actually knew and which risks you consciously accepted.
- **It converts open-ended doubt into a scheduled review.** When doubt appears, you can say: "My revisit conditions have not occurred. The next review is June."
- **It makes the decision a decision.** Writing it down closes the door in a way thoughts do not.

## Reversible vs. Irreversible: Spend Doubt Where It Belongs

Amazon's leadership has described a useful way of sorting choices: some are **one-way doors**, consequential and hard to reverse, and others are **two-way doors**, which you can walk back through. Different doors call for different amounts of doubt, and at different times.

| Type of door | Examples | When to do your doubting | After deciding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Two-way** (easy to reverse) | Trying a new gym, a schedule change, a draft email, a restaurant | Briefly, before | Act and adjust. Reconsidering is cheap, so use real feedback, not worry. |
| **One-way** (hard to reverse) | Signing a long lease, a major surgery, a big financial commitment | Thoroughly, before | Close it. The time for doubt was earlier, and more worry now can't change the outcome. |
| **Two-way but costly to revisit** | Changing jobs within the first year, moving cities | Moderately, before | Set a trial period and a review date, then honor them. |

The trap is doing the reverse: rushing a one-way decision and then agonizing over it for weeks, while analyzing small reversible choices for hours beforehand. If you notice yourself spending time on a decision you can't change, that is a signal to move to step 5 of the audit.

## Common Situations

**At work.** After sending a message or making a call, ask the new-information question before you reopen anything. If a colleague raises a real issue, that counts as new information and warrants a review. If you merely feel unsure, save the review for a set time, such as the end of the day. Waiting often gets you a more reliable answer than replaying it in the moment.

**In relationships.** Be careful here. "Is this the right person?" and "Did I say the wrong thing?" are different from "Did I pick the right cereal?" Real concerns about how you are treated or about shared values deserve attention, not a verdict from a ten-minute audit. But the audit still helps with the replay. After a conversation, you can ask: "Is there something I need to correct or clarify, or am I replaying?" If there is something to clarify, do it plainly. If not, let it go.

**On exams.** Use what the research suggests. Do not make a blanket rule such as "never change." Change an answer when you were unsure the first time *and* you now have a specific reason, such as noticing a word you missed or recalling a fact. Leave it alone when your only reason is a vague feeling.

**After you can't undo it.** Once a message is sent or a deadline has passed, the only useful question is "What do I do next?" Replaying the past can't change it.

**When you keep asking others.** Asking friends over and over for reassurance can give brief relief, but it tends to renew the doubt. Ask once, for information, not repeatedly for comfort.

## Some Small Tools That Help

- **A scheduled worry window.** Pick 15 minutes at a set time. When doubt appears outside the window, write it down and postpone it. Many people find that by the time the window opens, half the items are no longer pressing.
- **A friend's-advice reframe.** Ask "What would I say if a friend described exactly this decision and this doubt?" Research on [self-distancing and wise reasoning](https://uwaterloo.ca/wisdom-and-culture-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/grossmann_kross_-_exploring_solomons_paradox_-_article_proof.pdf) by Grossmann and Kross found that people reasoned more wisely about other people's problems than their own, and that taking a self-distanced perspective narrowed the gap.
- **A "good enough" definition.** Before you decide next time, write what "good enough" looks like: three must-haves and one dealbreaker. When an option meets them, stop.
- **A coin or the button as a mirror.** For a small, low-stakes choice, let a random answer decide, and notice whether you feel relief or disappointment. This can show your preference. You do not have to obey the answer. The point is the reaction.

## When It's More Than Everyday Doubt

Second-guessing is normal. But if it is constant, covers almost everything, takes up hours each day, or comes with intense distress, it may be more than a decision-making habit. Persistent doubt and repeated checking can be associated with anxiety and other conditions. If this sounds like you, talking to a doctor or mental health professional is a sensible step, and it does not mean something is wrong with you. This article is general information, not medical advice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do I second-guess everything I decide?

Several things can contribute: a habit of aiming for the best possible option (maximizing), a decision that stays reversible so your mind keeps working on it, and a memory bias that makes past mistakes from changing your mind feel more common than they were. A decision log and a clear "good enough" standard address all three.

### Is second-guessing yourself always bad?

No. Second-guessing based on new information is rational and healthy. The unhelpful kind is replaying a decision when nothing has changed. Research on test-taking found that changing answers helped more often than it hurt, so the goal is to second-guess for a reason, not to eliminate it.

### Should I trust my first instinct?

Not automatically. In the 1,561 exams studied by Kruger and colleagues, about 51% of answer changes went from wrong to right and about 25% from right to wrong, so the "first instinct" rule was not supported there. Life decisions have no answer key, though, so use the question "do I have a specific, new reason?" in place of "which came first?"

### How do I stop second-guessing myself in a relationship?

Separate real concerns from replaying. If something about how you are treated or about shared values keeps coming up, that deserves direct attention and probably a conversation. If you are replaying what you said or did, ask whether anything needs to be corrected. If so, address it plainly. If not, practice closing the thought. If the doubt is constant and distressing, consider speaking with a counselor.

### How long should I spend reconsidering a decision?

Give yourself a limited window, such as 15 minutes once, when there is genuine new information. Write down a verdict of hold, adjust, or reverse. Without a time limit and a written verdict, a review tends to turn into rumination.

### How do I stop second-guessing on exams?

Avoid both extremes. Do not refuse to change answers, and do not change on a hunch. Change an answer when you were unsure the first time and you now have a specific reason, such as a word you missed or a fact you remembered. If your only reason is a vague feeling, keep your first answer.

### When should I get help for constant self-doubt?

If doubt is constant, applies to most areas of life, uses up hours each day, or causes significant distress, consider talking to a doctor or mental health professional. Persistent doubt and repeated checking can be linked to anxiety and related conditions that are treatable.
