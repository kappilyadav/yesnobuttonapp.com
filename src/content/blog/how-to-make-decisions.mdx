Making decisions is hard, even the small ones. Standing in front of the fridge deciding what to eat, staring at two nearly identical job offers, or rereading the same text message for the tenth time before hitting send: these moments all draw on the same limited mental resource. Researchers call the drop in decision quality that follows a long stretch of choosing "decision fatigue," and it explains why the last decision of a long day so often feels harder than the first. The Yes No Button offers a deliberately low-tech fix for the small end of that problem: use randomness to break through indecision instead of grinding through it. Think of it as a [random decision maker](/) for the small stuff.

## Why a Coin Flip Actually Works

It sounds counterintuitive to hand a real decision over to a 50/50 random result, but there is genuine research behind the idea. University of Chicago economist Steven Levitt ran a large-scale study in which more than 20,000 people facing a difficult decision, from quitting a job to ending a relationship, flipped a virtual coin and were asked to follow it. People whose coin came up heads were meaningfully more likely to actually make the change than those who got tails, and the people who made a change reported being happier six months later, regardless of which way the coin had landed. Levitt's conclusion, published as an NBER working paper titled <a href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w22487" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">"Heads or Tails: The Impact of a Coin Toss on Major Life Decisions and Subsequent Happiness"</a>, was that most people are more cautious about making a change than is actually good for them. A coin, or a button, is a way of acting on that nudge.

The Yes No Button is a modern [yes or no coin flip](/), but it is not claiming to be a research instrument, and it should not carry the weight of major life decisions on its own. But the same underlying mechanism it runs on, a truly random 50/50 split, is exactly what that study used. What the coin (or the button) is really doing is not choosing for you. It is giving you a concrete outcome to react to, and your reaction is the real answer.

## The Psychology of Random Decisions

Here is the interesting part: when you get a result you do not like, you immediately know what you actually wanted. The button acts as a mirror for your true preferences rather than an oracle for the future.

Say you ask "Should I quit my job?" and the button says "Yes." Pay attention to your gut reaction in that first second. If you feel a flash of relief, that tells you something real. If you feel a spike of anxiety, maybe you are not actually ready, or maybe there is a specific fear worth naming before you decide anything. The random result does not know your situation. Your emotional response to it does.

## A Worked Example

Imagine you have narrowed a restaurant choice down to two options and have been going back and forth in the group chat for ten minutes. Assign one option to Yes and the other to No, press the button, and notice what you feel when the answer appears. If your stomach sinks a little at "Thai food," you already know you wanted pizza. The button did not make the decision. It just gave your preference somewhere to show up. The same trick works for smaller stakes decisions all day long: which task to start with, whether to take the call now or in ten minutes, whether to go for the run or skip it today.

## When to Use the Button

The Yes No Button works best for:

1. **Low-stakes decisions**: what to eat, what to watch, where to go
2. **Truly balanced options**: when both choices are genuinely close, so the outcome will not sting either way
3. **Breaking loops**: when you have been going back and forth on the same two options for longer than the decision deserves
4. **Creative blocks**: when you need a random prompt to get unstuck and start moving

## When Not to Use the Button

Some decisions deserve real deliberation, not a random split:

- Major financial investments
- Health and safety choices
- Decisions that significantly affect other people
- Anything irreversible with serious consequences

For decisions like these, a random result can still be useful as a diagnostic, the way it was in Levitt's study, but it should never be the final word. Talk to the people affected, get expert advice where it matters, and treat the button's answer as a prompt to notice your own reaction rather than an instruction to follow.

## The 5-Second Rule

Try this technique: set a 5-second timer before you press the button. If you have not decided by the time it goes off, press it. This keeps you from spiraling into endless back-and-forth while still giving your brain a fair shot at a quick, intuitive choice first. Often you will find yourself deciding within those five seconds anyway, and the button becomes unnecessary. That is the goal, not a failure of the method.

## Using History as a Journal

The Yes No Button keeps a running log of your last several results in your browser. Over time, that history can reveal a pattern worth noticing: are you asking the same question over and over, in slightly different words? Repeatedly returning to the same yes-or-no question is often a better signal than any single answer. It usually means you already know what you want to do and are looking for permission, not information.

## Quick Answers

**How do I make decisions faster?** Set a hard deadline before you start weighing options, even an arbitrary one like the 5-second rule above. Most delay comes from re-litigating the same two or three points over and over, not from missing information. A deadline forces you to act on what you already know.

**What if I'm still confused after the button gives an answer?** That's a signal, not a failure. Confusion after a random result usually means the two options weren't actually as close as you thought, or that a factor you haven't named out loud is doing the real work. Sit with the confusion for a minute and ask what it is you're actually unsure about, rather than pressing the button again for a different result.

## Sort the Decision Before You Spend Effort on It

Not every decision deserves the same amount of thought, and a lot of stress comes from treating small choices like big ones. A useful first step is to ask two questions: how hard is this to undo, and how much does it matter a year from now?

Amazon founder Jeff Bezos has described a similar idea, splitting decisions into one-way doors that are hard to reverse and two-way doors that you can walk back through if you change your mind. Most everyday choices are two-way doors: what to cook, which film to watch, which task to start first, whether to try a new route. For those, the cost of a slightly wrong choice is tiny, while the cost of agonizing is real. Spending thirty minutes to avoid a ten-minute mistake is a bad trade, and the [decision fatigue](https://thedecisionlab.com/bias/decision-fatigue/) that builds up over a day makes later choices worse, too.

Genuinely one-way decisions, such as signing a lease, changing careers, or making a large purchase, deserve research, advice, and time. They are not a job for a random button. Sorting honestly between the two kinds is half the skill.

## Maximizers, Satisficers, and the Cost of the Perfect Choice

Economist Herbert Simon coined the term ["satisficing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisficing) for choosing the first option that meets your needs rather than searching for the single best one. Psychologist Barry Schwartz, in his well-known TED talk ["The Paradox of Choice"](https://www.ted.com/talks/barry_schwartz_the_paradox_of_choice), later popularized the contrast between maximizers, who try to find the optimal choice every time, and satisficers, who settle on "good enough". His research and writing suggest that maximizers often end up less satisfied with their decisions, even when their results are objectively better, because they keep wondering about the options they passed up.

You do not need to read the research to see the pattern in your own life. If you have ever spent an hour comparing nearly identical options and then felt worse about the one you chose, you have met the cost of maximizing. A quick random answer is a practical way to practice satisficing: it tells you to pick one of two good options and move on, because the difference between them is smaller than the cost of deciding.

## How to Read Your Reaction to the Result

The most valuable part of using a random answer is what happens in the second after you see it. Three reactions are common, and each means something different:

- **Relief.** You wanted that answer all along. Go with it. You never needed the button to know, only to give yourself permission.
- **Disappointment.** You wanted the other answer. That is useful information, and you are free to follow it. The random result did its job by bringing your real preference to the surface.
- **A shrug.** You genuinely do not care. Then the decision was not worth the time you were spending on it, and the random answer is a perfectly good tiebreaker.

Try to notice the reaction before you start reasoning about it. Reasoning comes quickly and tends to defend whatever you already half-decided, so the first flicker is usually the honest one.

## Pair the Button With a Pros and Cons List

For decisions that sit in the middle, bigger than lunch but not life-changing, a short list and a random answer work well together. Write down the real pros and cons of each option, but give yourself a time limit of five to ten minutes. If one option clearly wins, take it. If the two come out roughly even, you have learned something important: the options are close enough that either one will probably be fine, and a random tiebreaker is a reasonable way to finish.

The list does not have to produce the answer. Its job is to make sure you have noticed the factors that matter, so the random step is only used when the real analysis has genuinely run out.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Asking again until you get the answer you like.** If you press three times, you already knew what you wanted. Take the hint and choose it.
- **Using it for high-stakes choices.** A random result is not a substitute for information. Health, legal, and major financial decisions deserve real advice.
- **Vague questions.** The button can only answer a clear yes or no. "Should I be happier?" is not a question it can help with; "Should I send that email today?" is.
- **Hiding behind the result.** The answer is a prompt for your own decision, not a way to avoid responsibility for it. You are always free to overrule it.
- **Never deciding anything yourself.** The point is to break stalls on small choices, not to hand over every decision. Use it for the cases where indecision costs more than a wrong answer.

## Using It for Group Decisions

Random answers are especially useful when several people cannot agree. A neutral outcome removes the sense that anyone is pushing their own preference, which makes it easier for everyone to accept the result. For the best effect, agree on the rule before pressing: which option is yes, which is no, and that the result is final for this one question. Deciding the rules first is what keeps the process feeling fair, and it prevents the familiar "best of three" argument that follows a result somebody dislikes.
