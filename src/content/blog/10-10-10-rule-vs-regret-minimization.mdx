Two decision tests keep coming up whenever people are stuck on something big. One is the **10-10-10 rule**: how will you feel about this in ten minutes, ten months, and ten years? The other is Jeff Bezos's **regret minimization framework**: imagine yourself at 80 and ask which choice you would regret.

Both are popular, both are free, and both fit in a few sentences. Most articles about them simply restate the idea and tell you to try it. This one does something different. It looks at what the underlying psychology says, including a recent partial replication that complicates the famous "you regret what you didn't do" claim, and then works out when each test helps, when it misleads, and how to use both without making your decision harder.

## The Short Answer

- **They fix different mistakes.** 10-10-10 corrects for being swept up in how you feel right now. Regret minimization corrects for letting fear keep you from doing something you want.
- **Use 10-10-10** for decisions with a loud short-term feeling: sending the message, having the hard conversation, making the purchase.
- **Use regret minimization** for big discretionary bets where the downside is survivable: a career change, a move, starting something.
- **Neither one handles risk of ruin.** If a bad outcome would be unrecoverable, check that first, before any imagining.
- **The evidence is thinner than the advice.** We could not find controlled studies of 10-10-10 itself, and the "inaction is regretted most in the long run" finding held up only partly when someone tried to replicate it.

## The 10-10-10 Rule

The method was introduced by Suzy Welch in her column in *O, The Oprah Magazine* and later in her 2009 book *10-10-10: A Life-Transforming Idea*. The idea is to run a decision through three questions: what are the consequences in **ten minutes**, in **ten months**, and in **ten years**?

The point is to pull you out of the moment. Many bad decisions come from a short-term feeling that looks like the whole picture: the satisfaction of sending an angry email, the relief of avoiding a conversation, the thrill of a purchase. Asking about ten months and ten years brings the later consequences into view.

The numbers are not literal. They stand in for "right away," "once the dust settles," and "in the long run," and you can substitute hours, weeks, or decades. That flexibility is useful in practice, but it also means the method is hard to test scientifically as written.

### What the research says

There is a real body of research on why a time-shift might help, even if none of it tests 10-10-10 directly:

- **We misjudge how long feelings last.** In a series of studies reported by Gilbert, Pinel, Wilson, Blumberg and Wheatley in 1998 on what they called [immune neglect](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/immune-neglect-a-source-of-durability-bias-in-affective-forecasting-8204), people overestimated how long they would be affected by future events. In one well-known example, assistant professors expected a tenure decision to affect their happiness for years, and those who had already been through one reported recovering faster. The proposed reason is that we neglect our own capacity to adapt.
- **We focus too narrowly on the event.** In [follow-up work on focalism](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Wilson%20et%20al%20(Focalism).pdf), Wilson and colleagues suggested that part of the problem is that we think only about the event and not about everything else that will fill our lives afterward.
- **Stepping back improves reasoning.** In [a 2014 study of Solomon's paradox](https://uwaterloo.ca/wisdom-and-culture-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/grossmann_kross_-_exploring_solomons_paradox_-_article_proof.pdf), Grossmann and Kross found across three experiments with 693 participants that people reasoned more wisely about other people's relationship conflicts than their own, and that taking a self-distanced perspective (describing themselves in the third person) eliminated that gap.

Put together, these studies support the spirit of 10-10-10: zooming out from the present moment tends to help. They do not show that these three particular time horizons are better than any others.

### Where it falls short

- **Your ten-year guess is also biased.** The same research that says feelings fade faster than we expect also means your forecast of how you will feel at ten months is unreliable. Use it as a prompt for thinking, not as a prediction.
- **It does not tell you what to value.** One commentary on the book notes that it does not specify the criteria for judging the consequences. If you do not know what matters to you, three time horizons will not tell you.
- **It can talk you out of everything uncomfortable.** The ten-minute column is often the honest one. A hard conversation really will feel bad for ten minutes, and sometimes the right call is to avoid a risk, not to push through it.

## The Regret Minimization Framework

Jeff Bezos has described using this approach in 1994, when he was working at the hedge fund D. E. Shaw and weighing whether to leave to start what became Amazon. He imagined himself at 80, looking back on his life, and asked which choice he would regret. He concluded that he would not regret trying and failing but would regret not having tried.

The logic mirrors a classic idea in psychology: in the long run, people regret what they did not do more than what they did. That is what makes the framework feel true. But is it?

### What the research says (and what happened when it was re-tested)

The classic source is Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec's 1994 work on the temporal pattern of regret. Their finding was that actions tend to produce more regret in the short term, while inactions tend to be regretted more in the long term. In one of the original studies, with 32 people, 84% named an inaction as their bigger long-term regret.

That was a small study. In 2023 a team ran [a large field replication at a science museum in Chicago](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10282588/) with 2,600 visitors. Here is what they found:

| Time frame | Chose action | Chose inaction | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Past few days (short term) | 58% | 42% | Significant majority for action |
| Life so far (long term) | 51% | 49% | No significant difference |

The key interaction between action versus inaction and time frame was still significant, so the basic idea held up. But the effect was much smaller than in the original (w = 0.065 versus 0.15), and the specific pattern differed. People did not show a long-run majority for inaction regrets. The authors called it a successful but partial replication and suggested the museum setting and a younger sample might have played a part.

A second body of evidence is Neal Roese and Amy Summerville's 2005 paper [What we regret most... and why](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2394712). Their meta-analysis of 11 datasets with 3,041 participants found the biggest regrets cluster in six areas: education, career, romance, parenting, the self, and leisure. Their explanation is the **opportunity principle**: we regret most where we still see room to do something about it. Notably, the authors say their own studies did not directly test action versus inaction, and they point out limits such as convenience samples (73% of participants were women).

### What this means for the framework

- **"Inaction hurts most" is a tendency, not a law.** The replication shows it is weaker than the popular version suggests. Do not treat the 80-year-old self as guaranteed to side with the bold option.
- **The opportunity principle gives you a better question.** If regret is strongest where open possibilities remain, then "which choice keeps more doors open?" may be a more useful prompt than "which would I regret?"
- **The test is one-sided.** It asks whether you will regret *not trying*. It does not ask whether trying could cause harm you cannot undo. That is how it gets misused: as a push toward leaps without a floor.
- **The Bezos story is a survivorship example.** Many people with the same instinct and the same framework did not end up with Amazon. The framework did not make the decision good by itself. His ability to take a risk with a bounded downside did much of that work.

## Which One to Use

| Your situation | Better test | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reacting to an annoying message, email, or comment | 10-10-10 | The problem is the present-moment feeling |
| Avoiding a hard conversation | 10-10-10, then be honest about the ten-minute column | The cost is brief; the benefit is lasting |
| A purchase you are tempted by | 10-10-10 | Separates the thrill from the lasting value |
| Changing careers, moving, starting a project | Regret minimization, with a ruin check | A big discretionary bet where fear may be the main brake |
| Deciding whether to say yes to a big commitment | Both | Short-term cost plus long-term meaning |
| Anything with a possible unrecoverable harm | Neither first. Check the downside, then decide | Imagination tests are weak against ruin |
| Two options that are really about equal | Neither. Pick | Time travel will not break a true tie |

## A Combined Check You Can Do in Ten Minutes

This works for a decision that matters but is not an emergency. Write short answers as you go.

1. **State the decision as a question,** such as "Should I accept the offer in the other city?"
2. **Do the ruin check.** What is the worst realistic outcome, and could I recover from it? If not, stop and deal with that first.
3. **Use the 10-10-10 columns for each option.** Write what you expect to feel and what will actually have happened at each point. Add one line for "what else will be going on in my life by then," which is the focalism fix.
4. **Use the 80-year-old question, with the opportunity twist.** For each option, ask which regret I could still do something about later, and which one would be permanent. Prefer the choice that is easier to recover from, unless a closed door is the whole point.
5. **Look for a smaller version.** Is there a trial, a pilot, a part-time version, or a delay that gives you most of the information at a fraction of the risk?
6. **Check the mirror.** Say your leading option out loud, or ask an impartial answer tool and watch your reaction. Disappointment or relief is data.
7. **Take the third-person view.** Write "If a friend told me this story, I would say…" Finish the sentence. The Solomon's paradox research suggests this is where better advice comes from.

### Two illustrative examples

**A harsh email.** You have just been criticised by a colleague and have a reply drafted. *Ten minutes:* sending it feels great. *Ten months:* the colleague still works with you, and the email may have become part of how they see you. *Ten years:* irrelevant, apart from your reputation for how you handle conflict. The ten-minute column is a temptation and the others are the cost. Send a shorter, calmer version tomorrow.

**An offer in another city.** There is a ruin check (savings for a few months, a way back), a 10-10-10 pass (the first months are lonely and expensive, the first year is the real test, ten years out you may have a very different life or may have returned), and the regret question (the opportunity to work on this kind of project may not come again). The answer might be yes, with a plan: negotiate a start date, a relocation allowance, and a six-month check-in. The framework helped by making the decision clearer, not by providing the answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the 10-10-10 rule for decision making?

It is a method from Suzy Welch's book *10-10-10* (2009) in which you ask how a decision will affect you in ten minutes, ten months, and ten years. It is designed to pull you out of a strong short-term feeling and put the longer-term consequences in view. The time frames are approximate and can be changed.

### What is the regret minimization framework?

It is a decision approach Jeff Bezos has described using when he left a hedge fund to start Amazon. You imagine yourself at 80 looking back and ask which choice you are more likely to regret. It is most useful for big discretionary choices, such as career moves, where fear may be holding you back.

### Is it true that people regret what they didn't do more than what they did?

Partly. Classic research found actions are regretted more in the short term and inactions more in the long term. A large 2023 field replication in a Chicago science museum confirmed the interaction between action, inaction, and time, but the effect was smaller and the pattern was different: in the long term, the split was roughly even. Treat it as a tendency, not a rule.

### Does the 10-10-10 rule actually work?

There are no controlled studies of 10-10-10 itself that we could find. Related research on affective forecasting, focalism, and self-distancing suggests that stepping back from the present moment tends to improve judgment. So the method is plausible and low-cost, but not proven as a technique.

### Which is better, 10-10-10 or regret minimization?

Neither is better overall. Use 10-10-10 when a strong immediate feeling is driving a decision. Use regret minimization for large, survivable bets. For anything with a risk of unrecoverable harm, check the downside first.

### What if the tests give me different answers?

That is normal and informative. A common pattern is that the ten-minute and ten-month columns favor the safe option while the 80-year-old question favors the bold one. Then ask whether a smaller version of the bold option exists, such as a trial period, that lets you test it without a leap.
