Yes, there are hundreds of apps that will "make a decision for you". The catch is that the phrase covers four very different jobs, and an app that is perfect for choosing a restaurant can be useless, or even harmful, for choosing a job. Most pages about this topic are lists of app names. This one is about what the apps actually do, which of your decisions are worth handing to them, and how to check an app before you let it onto your phone.

## The Four Kinds of "Decision Apps"

| Type | What it does | Good for | Weak spot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Random deciders** (yes/no button, coin, wheel, dice) | Gives an answer by chance, with no reasoning | Breaking ties, low-stakes choices, games | Knows nothing about your situation |
| **Comparison tools** (pros and cons, weighted scoring, decision matrix) | Helps you list options and score them | Choices with several factors, such as a laptop or a flat | You can tilt the scores toward what you already want |
| **AI advisors** (chatbot-style) | Talks through the problem and suggests an answer | Brainstorming options you had not thought of | Sounds confident whether or not it has the facts |
| **Prediction apps with money on the line** | Lets you stake real money on an outcome | Nothing to do with deciding | These are betting products, not decision tools |

The last row needs a short warning. Searches for "yes or no app" turn up a lot of prediction and betting apps where a yes or no answer is something you put money behind. A decision maker should never ask for payment details, and one that does is a different kind of product. Everything below is about the first three rows, with most of the attention on random deciders, because they are the simplest and the most misunderstood.

## A Random App Does Not Decide. It Reveals.

This is the idea that makes a yes/no app useful instead of silly. When a random answer lands, one of two things happens in the first two seconds: you feel relief, or you feel a small drop of disappointment. That reaction is information about what you wanted all along. The app did not decide anything. It gave you a fast, cheap way to hear your own preference.

The reaction test is a widely used technique rather than a proven one. The closest research is a large University of Chicago study, covered in our guide to [making better decisions](/blog/how-to-make-decisions/), where people facing a real choice followed a coin flip and those who made a change reported being happier six months later. It suggests many of us hold back from changes we would benefit from, and a random answer gives that a nudge. The way to use the idea on purpose is a short routine:

1. **Phrase one question so "yes" means doing the thing.** "Should I send the message?" works. "Should I not send it?" does not.
2. **Say out loud which answer you are hoping for before you press.** This takes five seconds and is the whole trick.
3. **Press once.**
4. **Notice your reaction before you read the answer as an instruction.** Relief means your preference matches. Disappointment means it does not, and you now know that.
5. **Act on what you learned, not necessarily on the answer.**
6. **Never press a second time for the same question.** A second press is not a second opinion. It is a search for the answer you wanted, and it makes the first reaction useless.

## Which Decisions to Hand to an App: A Three-Question Test

Not every decision should go near a random answer. Ask these three questions about the choice in front of you:

1. **Can I undo it cheaply?** Tonight's dinner, yes. A resignation letter, no.
2. **Would I be fine with either outcome?** If you would only be happy with one result, you are not undecided, you are hoping.
3. **Is more thinking costing more than the difference between the options?** If two choices are close in value, the time you spend comparing them is often worth more than the gap between them. This is the heart of [analysis paralysis](/blog/analysis-paralysis-why-you-cant-decide/), and one reason [decision fatigue](/blog/is-decision-fatigue-real/) builds up on days full of tiny choices.

| Your answers | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Yes to all three | Press the button and move on. What to eat, what to watch, who goes first. |
| Yes to two, and the third is "maybe" | Use it as a tie-break or a reaction test (the six steps above), not as the final word. |
| "No" to question 1 or 2 | Keep it out of the decision. Use a method built for it, such as the [10-10-10 rule](/blog/10-10-10-rule-vs-regret-minimization/) or a [better pros and cons list](/blog/pros-and-cons-list-not-working/). |

## Phrase the Question So a Random Answer Cannot Mislead You

The button is perfectly symmetric. Your question usually is not, and that is where people get fooled.

- **Make "yes" the harder action.** "Should I go for a run?" with a yes means the effort happens. "Should I skip my run?" with a yes means the easy thing wins, and you will feel that in the reaction.
- **One decision per press.** "Should I quit and move?" has two decisions hiding in it, and a "yes" tells you nothing about which one you wanted.
- **Keep it to two real options.** For three choices, use a three-way mode rather than pressing repeatedly until something you like appears.
- **Treat "maybe" as information.** If a three-way answer comes back "maybe", the choice is closer than you thought. Do not press again. Switch method: sleep on it, or apply the [10-10-10 rule](/blog/10-10-10-rule-vs-regret-minimization/).

If you are deciding between two options you like equally, the dedicated guide on [how to decide between two equally good options](/blog/how-to-decide-between-two-equally-good-options/) covers that case in more depth.

## Why Your History Will Look Biased, and Why It Is Not

Many apps keep a running tally of Yes and No answers. It is tempting to read patterns into it, and it is a good moment to know what randomness really looks like. These figures are exact for a fair 50/50 answer:

- In **10 presses**, there is about a **75% chance** that one answer shows up 6 or more times.
- In **10 presses**, there is about a **46% chance** of seeing the same answer 4 times in a row somewhere.
- In **50 presses**, a split of 29 to 21 or more lopsided happens about **32%** of the time.
- In **100 presses**, a split of 60 to 40 or worse happens about **6%** of the time. That is rare enough to be worth a second look, but not impossible.

## A Six-Point Check Before You Install Any Decision App on Android

Random deciders are simple apps, so they should be easy to vet. Before you install one, spend two minutes on this list:

| Check | How to test it in 30 seconds |
| --- | --- |
| **1. What data does it collect?** | Open its Google Play page and read the **Data safety** section. A decision button has no reason to collect your contacts, location or microphone. |
| **2. Does it work offline?** | Turn on airplane mode and press the button. If it fails or shows a loading spinner, it depends on a server. |
| **3. Does it show ads over the answer?** | Press it ten times. An ad that appears between your question and your answer is pressure at the exact moment you are most open to it. |
| **4. Does it demand an account?** | A random answer needs no login. A sign-up wall is a data-collection wall. |
| **5. Is it right for the people using it?** | Check the age rating, and for kids look for the Play Families Policy badge. |
| **6. Does the sound work for you?** | If an app reads answers aloud, media volume (not ringer volume) controls it, and the device's text-to-speech must be enabled in system settings. |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there an app that makes decisions for you?

Yes, in several forms: random deciders such as a yes/no button, coin or wheel, comparison tools that score your options, and AI advisors. A random decider gives you an answer by chance, a comparison tool organises your own reasoning, and an AI advisor suggests an answer from a conversation. None of them knows your situation as well as you do.

### Is there a decision maker app that works offline?

Some do. A simple random decider has no need for the internet, so a good one should keep working in airplane mode. Test it before you rely on it: switch on airplane mode and press the button. The Yes No Button app on Google Play states that it works fully offline.

### Are the answers from a yes/no app really 50/50?

For everyday use, yes. Each press is an independent random roll, so earlier answers do not affect later ones. Streaks and lopsided tallies are normal, as the numbers above show. These tools are not cryptographically secure, so they should not be used where someone might try to predict or rig the result, such as a lottery draw.

### Should I use an app for a big life decision?

No. Use it as a tie-breaker or a way to read your own reaction, never as the final word on health, legal, money or relationship choices. For high-stakes decisions, talk to people you trust and use a structured method such as the [10-10-10 rule](/blog/10-10-10-rule-vs-regret-minimization/).

### Is a yes/no app safe for kids?

It depends on the app, so check its age rating, its Data safety section and whether it shows ads. The Yes No Button listing on Google Play is rated for ages 3 and up, says no data is collected, and carries the Play Families Policy commitment. We also keep a [kids version of the tool](/kids-yes-no-button/) on the website.

### Why does my decision app not speak the answer?

First check that your **media volume** is up, because spoken answers do not follow the ringer volume. Then make sure a text-to-speech engine is installed and switched on in your phone's system settings, and that the phone is not in a silent or do-not-disturb mode that mutes media. In the Yes No Button app, also check that **Speak the answer** is switched on in Settings. If it is still silent, restart the app once.
