Choosing between a good option and a bad one is easy. The choices that eat whole evenings are the ones where both options are fine: two apartments, two job offers, two laptops, two ways to spend the weekend. You have made the pros and cons list, and it came out level. Nothing is left to learn, and you still cannot decide.

Most decision advice assumes there is a hidden right answer you can find by thinking harder. When two options really are close, that assumption is wrong, and thinking harder is the thing making you miserable. This guide gives you a way to work out whether you are facing a genuine tie, how much time the choice deserves, how to break the tie, and how to stop second-guessing afterwards.

## Why Ties Feel So Hard

If one option were clearly better, you would just pick it. The pain of a close call comes from a few separate things, and it helps to know which one you are dealing with.

**You are comparing different kinds of value.** Philosopher Ruth Chang argues that many hard choices are hard not because we lack information but because the options are *on a par*: one is better in some ways, the other is better in other ways, and neither is better overall. Cheaper rent against a shorter commute does not reduce to one number. Gathering more facts will not make the two collapse into a winner, because the difficulty is in how you weigh, not in what you know. You can watch her explain the idea in her TED talk, [How to make hard choices](https://www.ted.com/talks/ruth_chang_how_to_make_hard_choices).

**You are afraid of the road not taken.** When both options are good, choosing one means giving up something good. That loss is real even when the choice was fine.

**You are trying to be sure.** Some people want to be certain they picked the best option. Psychologist Barry Schwartz and colleagues called these people *maximizers* and contrasted them with *satisficers*, who pick something that clears their bar and move on. In a 2002 paper in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, maximizing tendency was linked to more regret and lower satisfaction with choices ([abstract on PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12416921/)). Later work questioned how well the maximizing scale was measured, so treat it as a useful idea rather than a settled fact. The practical point survives either way: demanding the guaranteed best option is often what makes a good choice feel bad.

**Your options are not actually tied.** Sometimes a "tie" is really one option you want and one you think you should want. That one is solved differently, and the next section shows how to check.

## Step 1: Check That It Is a Real Tie

Before you break a tie, make sure you have one. Try these three quick tests.

**The small-bribe test.** Imagine someone offers you a small bonus, such as 5% off or a free month, if you pick option B. Does that settle it immediately? If yes, B was already slightly ahead in your head. If the bonus changes nothing and you would still hesitate, the options are genuinely close.

**The disappointment test.** Imagine the decision is made for you: option A is gone and you must take B. Notice your first reaction before you start rationalising. Relief means you were leaning toward B. A sinking feeling means you were leaning toward A. A shrug means a real tie.

**The advice test.** Describe the situation to a friend and ask which they would pick. Pay attention less to their answer than to whether you feel a pull to argue with it. Wanting to push back shows what you actually prefer.

If any of these reveals a preference, you do not have a tie. You have a decision that is already mostly made, and the work now is to trust it. If all three come out level, move on to step 2.

## Step 2: Set a Time Budget Before You Deliberate

When two options are close, extra deliberation buys very little, because by definition the expected difference between them is small. The more time you spend, the more you pay for a small expected gain.

A useful rule is to set a deadline before you start, sized to two things: how costly a wrong choice is, and how easy it is to reverse. The table below is a rule of thumb, not research, but most people find it calibrates them quickly.

| Stakes | Reversible? | Reasonable time budget | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low | Yes | Under 5 minutes | Which restaurant, which film, which gift card |
| Low | No | An evening | A tattoo of a small design, a non-refundable trip |
| High | Yes | A few days | Moving cities for a year, a trial subscription, a short contract |
| High | No | Weeks, with real research | A house, a career change, having children |

For anything in the first row, a tie should end in minutes. If you notice you have spent a full evening choosing between two sets of headphones, the cost of that evening already exceeds any difference between them. The high-stakes, hard-to-reverse row is different: there, a genuine tie usually means you have not yet found the *deciding* information, and the time is better spent looking for it.

## Step 3: Pick a Tie-Breaker

Once you know it is a tie and you know your deadline, use a method. Here are the options, roughly from fastest to slowest.

### Flip a coin, and watch how you feel

This is the best-studied option, and it works in a slightly different way from how people expect. Economist Steven Levitt ran a large randomized field experiment in which people having difficulty making a decision flipped a coin to help determine their choice. The coin was randomly heads or tails, so its outcome can be treated as a cause rather than a symptom. Those told by the coin to make a change were more likely to make it, and when Levitt followed up, they were happier six months later than those whose coin told them to keep things as they were. His conclusion was that people may be excessively cautious when facing life-changing choices. The paper is [Heads or Tails: The Impact of a Coin Toss on Major Life Decisions and Subsequent Happiness](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22487), published in the *Review of Economic Studies* in 2021.

Two honest caveats. The participants were volunteers who chose to take part, and happiness was self-reported, so this does not prove a coin is a wise way to choose. It also tells you about people who were already stuck on a big decision, not about everyday choices.

The more useful takeaway is about *reaction*. The strongest technique is not to obey the coin but to use it as a mirror:

1. Assign each option a side. Heads is A, tails is B.
2. Flip.
3. **Before you look at the coin's verdict as an order, notice your first feeling.** A flash of relief or disappointment usually tells you which one you wanted.
4. If you feel relief, go with the coin. If you feel disappointment, go with the other option, because you just found your preference.

Either way the coin has done its job: it has turned a vague tie into a clear signal. You can use a physical coin, or the [Yes No Button](/) if you want a quick digital version. Ask it "Should I take option A?" and watch your own reaction to "Yes" or "No". If you are curious about how fair a coin or a digital flip really is, we cover it in [Is a Coin Flip Really 50/50?](/blog/is-a-coin-flip-really-50-50/)

### Take the option that is easier to undo

If one option can be reversed or adjusted more cheaply than the other, choose it. Keeping the door open has real value, and it makes a wrong choice cheaper. A one-year lease beats a three-year lease when you are unsure. A refundable ticket beats a non-refundable one.

### Take the option you would regret *not* trying

Ask which option you would more likely regret skipping in ten years, assuming it turned out merely okay. Research on regret (Gilovich and Medvec, 1995) suggests that over the long run people often regret what they did not do more than what they tried, so the question usually nudges you toward the option with more growth in it. It does not apply when the safe option protects something you cannot lose, such as your health or a dependent's stability.

### Pre-commit with a rule

For repeated tie decisions such as lunch, a weekend plan, or a gift, choose a rule once and use it every time: alternate, take the cheaper, or take the new one. A rule removes the daily negotiation entirely. Our guide to [settling what to eat tonight](/blog/what-should-we-eat-tonight/) shows several rules that work for dinner.

### Give each option a one-day trial

If the decision allows it, "live" in one option for a day. Plan your day as if you had picked A, then do the same for B. Do not analyse, just notice whether one day feels lighter. This works well for choices about where to live, which job to take, or which project to start.

## Step 4: Commit, Then Make It Good

The decision itself matters less than what you do next. When options are on a par, most of the final outcome is created *after* the choice by what you do with it. Three habits help.

**Write down why you chose it.** Even a sentence is enough: "I picked the second apartment because it is closer to my friends." It gives you something to read when doubt arrives, and it stops you re-running the whole debate.

**Stop comparing.** Ignore reviews of the other option, and do not check whether the other offer is still available. Looking back at the option you passed on gives regret a place to grow without telling you anything useful.

**Name what you gained.** Make a short list of the best things about the option you picked and use them. This is not self-deception. When two options are truly close, there is plenty of real good in either.

## When Not to Use a Coin

Randomness is a tool for ties, and a poor one for almost everything else. Do not flip a coin when:

- **The stakes are lopsided.** If one option risks your health, safety, or money you cannot afford to lose, it is not a tie, and a coin would be treating unequal things as equal.
- **One option is clearly better.** A coin will happily send you the wrong way. Check with the three tests in step 1.
- **Other people are affected and have not agreed to it.** Choices that involve a partner, child, or employee are not yours alone to randomise.
- **You are legally or medically bound.** Medication, legal filings, and safety decisions should follow professional advice, not luck.

For lighter choices, such as movies, meals, and weekend plans, a random answer is a fast way to remove friction. ## A Short Worked Example

This is an illustrative example. Priya has two job offers. Company A pays 4% more and is a 40-minute commute. Company B pays slightly less, is remote three days a week, and has a smaller team. She has spent a week on pros and cons.

- **Step 1.** The small-bribe test: if Company B offered 4% more, she would take B at once. So the pay gap is the only thing holding A up, and she suspects she is leaning to B. The disappointment test confirms it: imagining A is withdrawn, she feels relief.
- **Step 2.** Stakes are high but a job is semi-reversible. She gives herself until Friday.
- **Step 3.** She flips a coin with heads for A and tails for B. It lands on heads, and she feels her stomach drop. That is her answer: B.
- **Step 4.** She writes, "Choosing B for the flexibility and the team," accepts, and does not check what A is doing next month.

Notice that the coin never made the decision. It exposed a preference she had already formed and was ignoring.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do you do when two options are equally good?

First confirm it is a real tie using the small-bribe and disappointment tests. If it is, set a time limit based on how costly and reversible the choice is, then break the tie with a coin flip, the more reversible option, or a one-day trial of each. Afterwards, commit and stop comparing.

### Is it okay to flip a coin to make a decision?

For genuine ties and low-to-moderate stakes, yes. In a randomized field experiment, economist Steven Levitt found that people stuck on big decisions who were told to change by a coin toss were more likely to change and were happier six months later than those told to keep the status quo. The coin works best as a mirror for your reaction, not as an order, and it should never decide safety, medical, or legal matters.

### Why is it so hard to choose between two good options?

Often the options are on a par: each is better in different ways, so no extra information will reveal a winner. Fear of missing out on the other option and a wish to guarantee the best result make it worse. Recognising the choice as a tie lets you stop searching for an answer that does not exist.

### How long should I spend on a decision between two similar options?

Match the time to the stakes and reversibility. A low-stakes, easily reversed choice should take minutes. A high-stakes, hard-to-reverse choice such as a house or a career change deserves weeks and real research, but if the options still tie after that, the extra time is no longer buying you much.

### What if I regret my choice afterwards?

Some regret is normal, since choosing one option means giving up another good one. Writing down why you chose, avoiding comparisons with the option you passed up, and focusing on what you gained all reduce it. If the regret is strong and persistent, treat it as information and see whether the choice can be undone.

### Are maximizers really less happy than satisficers?

A 2002 paper by Barry Schwartz and colleagues found that people who strongly seek the best option reported lower happiness and more regret across several samples. Later researchers questioned how well the maximizing scale measures what it claims to, so the finding is suggestive rather than settled. Still, picking an option that is good enough and moving on is a reliable way to reduce second-guessing.
