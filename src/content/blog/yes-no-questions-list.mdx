This list exists to solve that specific problem: over 100 real yes or no questions, grouped into six everyday categories, each with a short note on when that kind of question tends to come up and why it is worth asking out loud instead of just turning it over in your head one more time.

This is a reference list for reading, browsing, and copying a question into the button when you need one. If you want something faster and more playful, the homepage has an interactive category picker where you tap a topic and a question fills in automatically. This page is the long-form version: more questions per category, and context for each one.

## Why Keep a Ready-Made List of Questions

The hardest part of a decision is rarely the decision itself. It is figuring out how to phrase the question in a way that actually captures what you are stuck on. A vague worry like "I don't know what to do about work" is nearly impossible to hand to a random generator. "Should I ask for a raise this quarter?" is not. Turning a fuzzy feeling into a specific yes-or-no question is most of the work, and having a list of proven phrasings on hand makes that step nearly instant. That is the whole value of a list like this one: it is a shortcut past the blank-page problem, not a replacement for thinking about what you actually want to ask.

## Make Any Question Go Deeper With Follow-Ups

A yes or no answer is a starting point, not the finish line. The real value often comes from a second question asked right after the first. Keep a few follow-ups in your pocket and use them when an answer seems worth exploring:

- **"What makes you say that?"** Invites the reasoning behind the answer without sounding like a challenge.
- **"What would change your answer?"** Reveals what the person actually cares about and where their answer is firm or flexible.
- **"On a scale of one to ten, how sure are you?"** Turns a binary answer into a gradient, which is often more honest.
- **"Has your answer changed over time?"** Opens a story rather than a position.

These work whether you are asking a friend, a partner, a colleague, or yourself in a journal. The idea is backed by research: the Greater Good Science Center's page on [36 questions for increasing closeness](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness) explains how taking turns answering gradually more personal questions helps people feel closer than small talk does. A "yes" followed by "why" says far more than the same "yes" alone.

## How to Write Good Yes or No Questions

If you want to build your own list, a few rules separate questions that spark good answers from ones that fall flat:

1. **Ask about one thing at a time.** "Do you like your job and your city?" has two answers hiding in one question. Split it.
2. **Keep the wording neutral.** "Don't you think remote work is better?" pushes the respondent toward a yes. "Do you prefer working remotely?" does not.
3. **Be specific.** "Are you happy?" is too broad to answer honestly in one word. "Did you enjoy this week?" is answerable.
4. **Make it answerable now.** Questions that depend on information nobody has produce shrugs, not insight.
5. **Match the question to the setting.** What works over dinner with close friends may not suit a team meeting.

These rules echo guidance from survey researchers. Pew Research Center's advice on [writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/our-methods/u-s-surveys/writing-survey-questions/) warns against leading wording and questions that ask about two things at once, which are exactly the problems the first two rules above avoid.

Here is how a weak question becomes a strong one:

| Weak | Stronger | Why it works better |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do you like food? | Would you eat the same meal every day for a week? | Specific and a little surprising |
| Are you ambitious? | Would you take a promotion that meant moving cities? | Tests a real trade-off |
| Is travel good? | Would you rather plan every detail of a trip than improvise? | Reveals a preference, not an opinion |

## Using the List for Journaling and Self-Reflection

You do not need anyone else to benefit from a list like this. Many people use yes or no questions as quick prompts for private reflection. A simple routine is to pick three questions at the start of the week, answer them honestly in a notebook or notes app, and add one sentence on why. At the end of the month, read back through your answers and look for patterns: questions you answer differently now, or ones where your answer is always the same.

Because each question has only two possible answers, journaling this way is fast enough to keep up even on busy weeks. It also takes the pressure off writing well. You are not producing an essay, only noticing what is true for you today.

## When "Maybe" Is the Honest Answer

Some questions on any list are genuinely hard to answer with a clean yes or no, and that is information too. If you find yourself wanting to say "it depends" or "sometimes", note what it depends on. That is usually the real subject worth discussing. If you want a tool that makes room for it, the [Yes No Maybe mode](/) adds a third outcome so uncertain questions have somewhere to land instead of forcing a choice you do not mean.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many yes or no questions are on this list?

This page has over 100 categorized questions across six everyday topics, with room to combine or adapt them to your own situation. Pair any of them with the [Yes No Maybe mode](/) if a question deserves a third, more nuanced option.

### Can I use these questions for journaling instead of the button?

Yes. Many people use this kind of list as a set of journal prompts rather than questions for the button itself. Pick one, write down your gut answer before you think too hard about it, then write a sentence about why. The list works the same way whether the "answer" comes from a button or from your own pen.

### Are these questions appropriate for kids?

Most of the daily decisions and games categories work well for younger audiences. For a version built specifically with families and kids in mind, see the [Kids Yes No Button](/kids-yes-no-button/), which has its own set of simple, playful questions.

### Do I have to use the exact wording from this list?

No. These are starting points. The best yes or no question is the specific one that's actually on your mind, phrased in your own words. Use this list to see the pattern, then write your own version if none of these quite fit.

### Do these questions come with answers?

No, and that's on purpose. This isn't a quiz with a fixed answer key: it's a list of prompts you feed into a random 50/50 result. The "answer" is whatever the button lands on, and the more useful signal is your own gut reaction to that result.
