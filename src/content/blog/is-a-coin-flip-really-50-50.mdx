A coin flip is the universal symbol of a fair, 50/50 chance. It decides kickoffs, settles arguments, and starts football matches. But is it actually fair? The short answer is: almost, but not exactly. Physicists and statisticians have shown that a normally flipped coin has a small built-in bias, and a large 2023 experiment measured it.

The more interesting question is whether that bias matters, and it is not the one most articles answer. This guide explains what the research found, why the bias exists, whether it should change how you flip, what the odds of landing on the edge really are, and how a digital yes or no button compares with a physical coin.

## The Short Answer

- A coin that is caught in the hand tends to land on the **same side it started on**, slightly more than half the time. The best estimates are around **51%**, not 50%.
- The bias is not about heads versus tails. It is about **which side faces up before the flip**. A heads-up start favours heads and a tails-up start favours tails.
- For everyday decisions, **the bias is far too small to matter**. It only matters if one person knows the starting side and the other does not.
- A fair procedure removes the problem completely, and it takes ten seconds. Skip to [how to flip fairly](#how-to-flip-fairly).
- A computer-generated yes or no has no starting side at all, which is one reason it is a good fit for quick decisions.

## Where the 51% Comes From: The Physics

In 2007, mathematician and magician Persi Diaconis, together with Susan Holmes and Richard Montgomery, published a paper titled [Dynamical Bias in the Coin Toss](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/S0036144504446436) in *SIAM Review*. They treated a coin toss as a physics problem rather than a probability problem, and noticed something simple: a flipped coin is not a random object. It is a spinning rigid disc, and its motion is fully determined by how it is launched.

To prove the point, they built a mechanical coin flipper that could launch a coin the same way every time. With the machine, the coin landed on the same face every time. A coin toss is predictable if you control the launch, which means the randomness in a real flip comes from variation in the thumb, not from the coin.

Then comes the subtle part. When a person flips a coin, the coin does not spin perfectly about a horizontal axis. It wobbles slightly, like a spinning top, which physicists call *precession*. The wobble means the coin spends a little more of its flight with its starting face pointing up. The authors' model predicted that a coin caught in the hand would land on its starting side about **51%** of the time.

That prediction stayed a theory for 16 years, because proving a 1% bias takes an enormous number of flips.

## The 2023 Experiment: 350,757 Flips

In 2023, a team led by František Bartoš at the University of Amsterdam set out to test it. Volunteers flipped coins from 46 different currencies, filmed each flip, and recorded whether the coin landed on its starting side. Altogether they collected **350,757 flips**, including a 12-hour flipping marathon. The results were posted as the preprint [Fair coins tend to land on the same side they started](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04153), and the university summarised them in [Heads or tails: pure chance?](https://www.uva.nl/en/content/news/news/2023/10/heads-or-tails-pure-chance.html)

What they found:

| Finding | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Same-side landing rate | About 50.8% |
| Diaconis model's prediction | About 51% |
| Preference for heads or tails | None: the bias follows the starting side |
| Differences between people | Large: some flippers showed almost no bias, others considerably more |

Two details are worth stressing. First, the match with the 2007 prediction is strong evidence that the wobble explanation is correct. Second, the bias depended on the person. Some flippers were close to perfectly fair, and others were well above average, which fits the idea that the effect comes from technique. When the preprint was first reported, it had not yet been peer reviewed, so treat the exact figures as the best current estimate rather than the last word.

## Does a 0.8% Bias Actually Matter?

For almost every real-life use, no.

A 50.8% chance means that in 1,000 flips, you would expect about **508** same-side results instead of 500. In a single flip, you cannot see it, and you cannot exploit it without knowing the starting side, which most coin flips do not reveal in a useful way.

It would matter in a narrow set of situations:

- **Repeated high-stakes flips**, where a small edge compounds. Sports leagues that decide things by coin toss do not care, because each flip is a single event.
- **One person holding information the other lacks.** If the flipper knows the coin started heads-up and also knows about the bias, they could call "heads" with a tiny edge. The researchers point out that this is why the starting side should be kept secret in important decisions.
- **Betting on streaks or long runs**, where you can estimate the bias accurately.

In short: the coin is not a perfect 50/50, but it is a very good one. The right takeaway is not "never trust a coin" but "do not let one person control the setup".

## How to Flip Fairly

You can make a coin flip fair even if the coin itself is slightly biased. These steps take about ten seconds.

1. **One person flips, the other calls in the air.** If the caller chooses after the coin is already spinning, the flipper cannot choose the starting side to favour the call.
2. **Let it land on the floor or table instead of catching it.** The Diaconis model describes the coin in flight, so a bounce adds extra unpredictability. This is a sensible precaution rather than something the studies have tested.
3. **Start heads-up one time and tails-up the next, or let the other person place the coin.** Keeping the starting side unknown or alternating removes the bias from the outcome.
4. **Agree on the rules first.** What counts as a valid flip, what happens if the coin rolls away, and what happens if it lands on its edge. We cover that last case below.
5. **Make the bet before the flip, not after.** Deciding what each side means *after* the coin lands is how you end up rationalising.

## Can a Coin Land on Its Edge?

It can, and people search for this constantly. A coin that lands standing on its edge is real but extremely rare for a normal flip onto a hard surface. A frequently quoted estimate, from physicists Daniel Murray and Scott Teare, is roughly **1 in 6,000** for a US nickel, and it depends mostly on the coin's thickness compared with its width: a thicker coin is more likely to stand. A thin coin makes it rarer. A later modelling study of a 50 euro-cent coin reached a similar order of magnitude, "one against several thousand", under normal flipping conditions.

So, for any practical purpose, treat a flip as having two outcomes. If a coin does land on its edge, re-flip. Agree on that before you start.

## Spinning a Coin Is Not the Same as Flipping It

Many people spin a coin on a table instead of flipping it, and the physics is very different. A spun coin has no wobble advantage, but it has a different problem: the weight is not distributed evenly, because one side has more raised metal than the other. Diaconis has reported that spun pennies can come up tails far more often than heads, with some coins showing a bias as large as roughly 80% according to a Science News account. Spinning is a **much less fair** way to decide something than flipping. If you must spin, use a coin you have tested.

## What About a Digital Yes or No?

A digital button works differently from a coin, and it is useful to see where it is fair and where it is not.

**There is no starting side.** A coin's bias depends on a physical launch. A computer picks its answer from a number generator, so the "same side" effect has no equivalent.

**It is pseudo-random, not truly random.** Browsers use a pseudo-random number generator (PRNG), an algorithm that produces numbers that behave statistically like random ones. Our [Yes No Button](/) calls the standard browser function `Math.random()` and treats a result below 0.5 as one answer and the rest as the other. In most browsers, this function is built on a fast generator in the xorshift family. It passes the ordinary statistical tests that matter for yes or no decisions.

**It is not cryptographically secure.** `Math.random()` is not designed to be unpredictable to an adversary. That is why browsers also provide `crypto.getRandomValues()`, which should be used for passwords, tokens, and anything where a determined person might try to guess the next value. For choosing a film, a restaurant, or who goes first, the difference is irrelevant. For a lottery with real money, you should use a cryptographically secure source, and you should not use our button.

**In "Yes, No, Maybe" mode, the three answers are about equal.** The button divides the number line into three roughly equal parts, so each of the three answers comes up about a third of the time.

**A digital result cannot be rigged by technique.** Nobody can flick their thumb to push it. That is a small but real advantage for settling arguments, because there is no technique to accuse the other person of.

The honest summary: a physical coin has a tiny and *knowable* bias that depends on technique, and a standard digital yes or no has a bias too small to measure for decision purposes. Neither deserves your worry for everyday choices.

## A Quick Way to Test a Coin Yourself

If you are curious whether your own coin or flipping style is biased, you can test it, but you will need a lot of flips. The reason is statistical: to detect a 1% bias reliably you need on the order of tens of thousands of flips, which is why it took until 2023 for the effect to be measured.

For a quick sanity check on a much larger bias, for example a worn or altered coin, flip it 100 times and count. A fair coin will usually give between 40 and 60 of one result. Counts outside that range are worth a second look, and counts outside 35 to 65 are very unlikely from a fair coin. This test cannot find a 1% bias, but it can catch a coin that is obviously unfair.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a coin flip really 50/50?

Almost, but not exactly. A coin that is flipped and caught tends to land on the same side it started on about 51% of the time, according to a 2007 physics model, and a 2023 experiment with 350,757 flips measured about 50.8%. The bias follows the starting side, not heads or tails, and it is too small to matter in everyday decisions.

### Which side is more likely to win a coin flip?

Neither heads nor tails is favoured. The side that faces up before the flip is slightly more likely to be facing up when the coin lands. If you do not know the starting side, you cannot use the bias, so the flip is effectively fair for you.

### Why does a coin land on the same side it started?

Because a flipped coin wobbles slightly instead of spinning perfectly end over end. According to the Diaconis, Holmes and Montgomery model, that wobble means the coin spends a little more of its flight with its starting face pointing up, which gives that face a small edge.

### How can I make a coin flip fair?

Have one person flip and the other call in the air, so the flipper cannot choose a side to suit the call. Let the coin land on a surface instead of catching it, alternate or hide the starting side, and agree on the rules, including what to do if the coin lands on its edge, before you flip.

### Can a coin land on its edge?

Yes, but it is very rare on a hard, flat surface. One commonly cited estimate puts it at about 1 in 6,000 for a US nickel. If it happens, re-flip, and agree to do so beforehand.

### Is a digital coin flip or yes no button fair?

For everyday decisions, yes. A browser-based button uses a pseudo-random number generator, which has no starting side and no human technique to bias it. It is not cryptographically secure, so it should not be used for anything where someone might try to predict or manipulate the result, such as a lottery or a security token.

### Is spinning a coin fair?

No. A spun coin is influenced by uneven weight distribution, and some coins show a large bias toward one side. Flipping is a much fairer method than spinning.
