Most lists of questions for friends are long, random, and the same everywhere. This one is organized around two things that decide whether a question lands: **how well the group knows each other** and **what mood you are in**. A question that is a delight at a loud party can be awkward on a quiet road trip, and one that is perfect for close friends can stall a table of acquaintances.

Below are 100 yes or no questions in five groups, from easy warm-ups to questions about the friendship itself. After the lists you will find four game formats with real rules, follow-up prompts that turn a one-word answer into a story, and ground rules that keep teasing friendly.

## How to Pick the Right Group

| Situation | Start with | Then move to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mixed group, new friends | Easy Warm-Ups | Hot Takes and Friendly Arguments |
| Party or game night | Hot Takes | Group Nights |
| Road trip or long drive | Group Nights | Questions About Us |
| Two or three close friends | Questions About Us | Deeper Questions |
| Group chat, one a day | Warm-Ups or Hot Takes | Anything with a clear yes or no |

A good order is light to deep, never the reverse. Opening with a heavy question puts everyone on guard, while a funny start makes the later honest ones feel safe.

## Four Ways to Play

A list is only a prompt. These formats turn it into a game that works with two people or ten.

### 1. Guess My Answer

One person is in the spotlight. They pick a question and write their answer secretly (or tell one neutral person). Everyone else predicts yes or no. Each correct guess scores one point, and the spotlight player scores one point for every person who guessed wrong. Rotate. It rewards knowing your friends and produces the best reactions, because someone is always certain that someone else would say yes.

### 2. Odd One Out

Everyone answers at the same time on a count of three, with a thumbs up or thumbs down. Answering together matters: when people hear others first, they tend to follow the room. If one person is alone in their answer, they explain. If everyone matches, move on quickly.

### 3. The Button Decides

Ask the question, then press the [Yes No Button](/). If it says **yes**, answer honestly. If it says **no**, you must argue the opposite of your real answer for thirty seconds, and the group votes on whether you were convincing. It is funny with the hot takes and surprisingly revealing with the deeper ones, because people often find they believe more of the opposite view than they expected.

### 4. The "And Why?" Ladder

Pick one question and keep asking one follow-up at a time: *Since when? What changed? What would convince you otherwise? Who else would say yes?* Three steps is usually enough. This is the slow format for a small group and a long evening.

## Follow-Ups That Get Real Answers

A bare yes or no gives you almost nothing. These follow-ups get the story behind it:

- **"Since when?"** shows how a habit or view started.
- **"What's the story?"** invites a memory, and memories are what people enjoy telling.
- **"Would past-you have said the same?"** works for deeper questions without sounding like therapy.
- **"Who here would answer differently?"** pulls the group in and starts a debate.
- **"What would change your mind?"** separates a strong view from a habit.

Avoid "why would you do that?" It sounds like judgment, and people stop answering honestly.

## Why Simple Questions Help Friendships

There is real research behind the idea that taking turns with questions builds closeness. A [meta-analysis of self-disclosure and liking](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/) by Collins and Miller (1994) found that people who share more intimate information tend to be liked more, that people tend to share more with those they already like, and that people come to like those who share with them. The effects depend on the type of study, the kind of disclosure and who is disclosing, so it is a tendency, not a guarantee.

A well-known practical version is the [36 questions for increasing closeness](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness) from the Greater Good Science Center. Its key design choice is gradual escalation: questions begin light and get more personal over time, and both people answer. That is the same logic as the order of the sections above. Treat the research as a reason to go slowly, not as a promise that a question game will fix a friendship.

## Reading the Answers (Without Over-Reading)

- **A quick, confident answer** usually means it is an easy topic. Do not push.
- **A pause** can mean the person is thinking, not hiding something. Give it a few seconds.
- **A joke instead of an answer** often means "not now." Let it go, and come back later if it matters.
- **A surprising yes** is the best moment in the game. Follow it up with curiosity.

A yes or no is one data point. Do not turn one answer into a verdict on someone's character.

## Ground Rules That Keep It Friendly

Friends know each other's soft spots, so teasing can slip into something else. Agree on four rules before you start:

1. **Anyone can pass,** no explanation needed.
2. **No judging.** React with curiosity or laughter, not criticism.
3. **What's said stays here,** especially embarrassing or personal answers.
4. **Stop if it stops being fun.** Switch groups or switch games.

Some questions in *Questions About Us* ask for honest feedback ("Have you ever been annoyed with me and not said anything?"). Use them only with friends who can take the answer, and thank the person for being honest before reacting.

## Playing Online and in Group Chats

- **Video calls:** ask a question, count down from three, and everyone gives a thumbs up or down on camera at once.
- **Group chats:** post one question a day. Replies can be a yes or no and a reason if people feel like it.
- **Polls:** most messaging apps have a poll feature that suits yes or no questions and shows the group result.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are good yes or no questions to ask friends?

The best ones match the mood and how well you know each other. Easy warm-ups like "Have you ever Googled yourself?" work with anyone. Hot takes like "Is a hot dog a sandwich?" start friendly debates. Questions about the friendship, like "Would you tell me if I had something stuck in my teeth?", suit close friends.

### How do you play yes or no questions with friends?

Take turns asking a question and have everyone answer. For a game, try Guess My Answer, where the group predicts one person's answer and scores points, or Odd One Out, where everyone answers at once and the person who disagrees with the group explains. The Yes No Button can decide who answers or force someone to argue the opposite.

### What should I do after someone answers yes or no?

Ask one follow-up, such as "Since when?", "What's the story?" or "What would change your mind?" A yes or no on its own tells you little. The follow-up is where the conversation starts.

### Are these questions good for a group chat?

Yes. Post one question a day, and people can reply with yes or no and a reason if they want. Warm-ups and hot takes work best. Save the deeper questions for in-person or small-group chats.

### How can I use the Yes No Button with these questions?

Use it to pick a random number from the list, decide who answers next, or run The Button Decides game, where a no means you must argue the opposite of your real answer for thirty seconds.
